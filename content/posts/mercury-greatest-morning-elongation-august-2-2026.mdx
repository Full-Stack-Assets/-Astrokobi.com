As summer deepens in the Northern Hemisphere, a bright speck will slip into the dawn sky, marking Mercury’s greatest morning elongation. On August 2 2026 the planet will sit 19° away from the rising Sun, reaching magnitude +0.1 and becoming one of the easiest inner‑planet targets before sunrise. This brief window offers both casual stargazers and seasoned observers a chance to watch the innermost planet dance away from solar glare. Spotting Mercury now also sets the stage for a close encounter with Jupiter later in the month.

<Callout type="takeaway">Mercury will be at its farthest apparent distance from the sunrise on August 2 2026, making it the optimal morning‑sky target.</Callout>

## What happened

Mercury completed its inferior conjunction on July 13 2026, passing directly between Earth and the Sun. Since then it has been moving ahead of Earth in its 88‑day orbit, re‑emerging in the eastern morning sky in mid‑July.

At 08:00 UTC on August 2 2026 (03:00 CDT) Mercury reaches greatest morning elongation, sitting 19° east of the Sun and shining at magnitude +0.1. Through a telescope the planet will appear about 39 % illuminated and 7.56 arcseconds across.

The planet continues to brighten, reaching magnitude ‑1.6 later in the month, and on August 15 it will pass within 0.6° of Jupiter about half an hour before sunrise, a pairing easily spotted with binoculars.

## Why it matters

Greatest elongation events are the most reliable times to view Mercury without a solar filter, offering a clear test of naked‑eye and modest‑instrument skills. For educators, the event provides a live illustration of orbital mechanics and the geometry of inferior conjunctions. Amateur astronomers can also use the timing to calibrate equipment and plan future observations of inner‑planet transits.

## How to think about it

First, check your local sunrise time and aim to look 30–45 minutes before it, when the sky is still dark enough for Mercury’s +0.1 magnitude to stand out. Use a wide‑field binocular or a small telescope pointed at the eastern horizon, aligning with the direction of sunrise. Mark the planet’s position relative to the waxing crescent moon on August 11 for an additional reference point, and keep an eye on the August 15 Jupiter conjunction for a rewarding binocular view.

<Callout type="warning">Never stare at the Sun or point optical instruments toward it; keep your aim well below the solar disk while searching for Mercury.</Callout>
