On the night of August 15, 2026, Venus will reach its greatest evening elongation, appearing farthest from the Sun in our sky. At 6 UTC (1 a.m. CDT) the planet will sit 46° west of the sunset point, shining at magnitude ‑4.4. This event makes Venus the most prominent object in the western twilight, visible to the naked eye and through modest telescopes. Observers will be able to track its slow drift toward the Sun until it disappears around late October.

## What happened
The greatest elongation occurs at 6 UTC on August 15, 2026, when Venus reaches an angular separation of 46 degrees from the Sun. At this moment the planet’s apparent magnitude is –4.4, making it brighter than any star and rivaling the Moon’s brightness in twilight. Through a telescope Venus will be about 49 % illuminated, resembling a first‑quarter phase, and will span roughly 24.48 arcseconds.

Elongations vary between about 45.4° and 47.3° depending on the year; the 46° separation this August is near the average maximum. After the peak, Venus will gradually slide closer to the sunset horizon, eventually slipping behind the Sun at inferior conjunction on October 24, 2026. The planet remains visible in the evening sky through October, offering a long window for casual and scientific observation.

## Why it matters
Venus’s extreme brightness and low evening altitude create a rare, low‑light‑pollution showcase for both casual stargazers and amateur astronomers. Its near‑first‑quarter phase reveals surface details such as cloud bands and the planet’s terminator, providing a hands‑on lesson in planetary phases and orbital geometry. Because the event spans several weeks, educators can plan outreach activities that connect skywatching with lessons on orbital mechanics and the inner solar system.

## How to think about it
Plan ahead with a free planetarium app such as Stellarium to locate Venus’s exact azimuth and altitude for your location. Aim for a viewing window 30–90 minutes after sunset, when Venus is high enough above the horizon to clear most ground‑level haze but still well before it sinks toward the western horizon. Choose a dark site with minimal light pollution, and use binoculars or a modest telescope (8‑10 in) to appreciate the 49 % illuminated disk and crisp terminator. Remember that Venus never rises in the midnight sky; its appearance is confined to the western twilight during this apparition.

<Callout type="warning">Never point any optical instrument at the Sun while searching for Venus, and never observe through a telescope when the Sun is above the horizon.</Callout>
