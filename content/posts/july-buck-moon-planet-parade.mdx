Look up tonight and you’ll see the July full Buck Moon rising alongside a line‑up of bright planets. Venus will shine as an evening star, followed by Saturn, Mars and Mercury as they trail the moon across the summer sky. This celestial procession offers a rare, easy‑to‑spot showcase for both casual observers and seasoned stargazers. The event highlights the interplay of lunar illumination and planetary positions, reminding us how dynamic our night sky truly is. With clear horizons, anyone can experience this mini‑planet parade without special equipment.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Buck Moon’s rise tonight creates a bright, easily observable planetary parade for all skywatchers.</Callout>

## What happened
The July full moon, traditionally called the Buck Moon, reaches its exact phase at 10:36 a.m. EDT on July 29 and will rise shortly after sunset, appearing larger near the horizon because of the Moon Illusion.

Venus will be the first bright object to the west after twilight, acting as the opening act for the procession. Within an hour, Saturn, Mars and Mercury will climb in the east, forming a line that follows the moon as it climbs higher.

A modest pair of binoculars (15× magnification) can enhance the view of the moon’s terminator and the planets, but a simple naked‑eye sighting is already impressive.

## Why it matters
The alignment offers a natural lesson in orbital mechanics and the way Earth’s rotation brings celestial bodies into view together. For educators, it provides a vivid, real‑time illustration of planetary motion that can spark curiosity in classrooms. Photographers and hobbyists gain a rare, low‑light opportunity to capture multiple bright objects in a single frame, enriching public appreciation of the night sky.

## How to think about it
First, locate a clear view of the southeastern horizon just after sunset to catch the moon’s rise. Use a star‑chart app to confirm the positions of Venus, Saturn, Mars and Mercury as they appear. If you have binoculars, steady them on a tripod and start with the moon’s terminator, then sweep eastward to spot the planets. Remember that the planets will rise later, so give them a few minutes to climb above any low‑lying obstacles.

<Callout type="warning">Never point any optical instrument at the Sun, and always use a stable support when using high magnification to avoid eye strain.</Callout>
