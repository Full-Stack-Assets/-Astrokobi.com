The Bijagós Archipelago off Guinea‑Bissau’s coast is a living laboratory where the sea’s pulse reshapes the land every six hours. NASA’s Earth Observatory highlights how the archipelago’s 88 islands and islets transform from exposed mudflats at low tide to submerged mangrove forests at high tide. This tidal rhythm sustains massive numbers of migratory shorebirds, sea turtles, and marine mammals, making the region a UNESCO World Heritage site of global importance. Understanding this dynamic helps scientists gauge how coastal ecosystems respond to climate‑driven sea‑level changes.

<Callout type="takeaway">The twice‑daily tides create a dynamic landscape that fuels one of West Africa’s most productive wildlife hotspots.</Callout>

## What happened
The research describes a maze of sandy channels, mudflats and mangrove forests that expand and contract with each tide, exposing intertidal zones that support an estimated 870,000 migratory shorebirds along the East Atlantic Flyway. During high tide, manatees, dolphins and schools of fish retreat into deeper waters while tens of thousands of green sea turtles use the exposed beaches for nesting, especially on Poilão Island. A 2025 analysis linked the archipelago’s tidal range—up to 7 meters (23 feet)—to the wide, shallow continental shelf and the geometry of the estuary, creating one of the largest tidal ranges in West Africa.

## Why it matters
The sheer abundance of birds, turtles and marine mammals makes the Bijagós a keystone for regional biodiversity and a critical stopover for migratory species. Protecting this habitat safeguards food webs that extend far beyond the islands, influencing fisheries and coastal economies across West Africa. Moreover, the extreme tidal fluctuations provide a natural laboratory for studying how climate change‑driven sea‑level rise could alter intertidal ecosystems worldwide.

## How to think about it
When evaluating coastal conservation priorities, weigh the frequency and magnitude of tidal exposure against the resilience of key species. Protecting mangrove buffers and limiting disruptive activities during low‑tide windows can preserve feeding grounds for birds and safe passage for hatchlings. Integrating satellite monitoring of tidal patterns with on‑the‑ground surveys will help managers anticipate habitat shifts as sea levels rise.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that the archipelago’s high biodiversity guarantees species survival without active protection and sustainable management.</Callout>
