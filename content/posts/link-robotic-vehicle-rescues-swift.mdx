A dramatic video captured from Puerto Rico shows NASA’s experimental repair craft LINK flying in lockstep with the aging Swift observatory. The two spacecraft, separated by only a minute in orbit, now share a similar trajectory after a software update steadied LINK’s wobble. Swift, launched in 2004 to study gamma‑ray bursts, has been losing altitude and faces atmospheric burn‑up by the end of 2026. If successful, LINK’s boost will lift Swift into a higher orbit, preserving a unique window on the most energetic explosions in the universe. The sighting underscores how commercial agility can extend the life of priceless scientific assets.
<Callout type="takeaway">LINK’s restored stability puts Swift’s rescue on track, buying years of gamma‑ray science.</Callout>
## What happened
NASA’s Swift Observatory has been spiraling lower as atmospheric drag drags it toward a 300 km decay altitude, a threshold that would cause it to burn up in Earth’s atmosphere. To avert loss, NASA contracted Katalyst Space to launch the commercial robotic repair vehicle LINK on July 3, 2026, with the goal of rendezvousing, attaching, and boosting Swift’s orbit.
Three weeks into the mission, LINK suffered failures in two of its three reaction wheels, causing an uncontrolled spin and intermittent communications. Engineers uploaded a new flight‑software patch on August 11 that re‑engineered the attitude control algorithms to use the remaining actuators, stabilizing the vehicle.
Amateur astronomers from the Sociedad de Astronomía del Caribe in Añasco, Puerto Rico, recorded video on August 10 showing both LINK and Swift traversing the sky within seconds of each other, indicating they are now on a near‑identical trajectory.
## Why it matters
Swift’s data on gamma‑ray bursts remain irreplaceable for understanding the most violent cosmic events, and extending its mission preserves a continuous record that would otherwise be lost. Demonstrating a successful on‑orbit repair using a commercial platform showcases a cost‑effective alternative to building new satellites, potentially reshaping how agencies manage aging assets. The operation also provides real‑world validation of autonomous rendezvous and docking technologies that could be repurposed for future debris‑removal or satellite‑servicing missions.

## How to think about it
When evaluating an aging satellite, weigh the remaining scientific value against the cost and risk of a rescue versus replacement. Identify whether a commercial partner can provide a rapid, low‑cost servicing option and assess the spacecraft’s remaining propellant and attitude‑control health. Build contingency plans that include software‑only fixes, as they can sometimes recover functionality without hardware repairs.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the rescue is guaranteed; further actuator failures or software anomalies could still jeopardize the mission.</Callout>
