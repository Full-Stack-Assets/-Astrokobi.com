The European space economy saw a significant boost in 2025, with government space spending jumping 12% to 13.5 billion euros ($15.4 billion), according to a report by the European Space Agency (ESA). This growth comes as global government space spending experienced a 3% decline, largely due to lower U.S. defense spending and flat NASA funding. The increase in Europe's space spending is attributed to rising national defense budgets.

<Callout type="takeaway">European government space spending increased by 12% in 2025, reaching 13.5 billion euros ($15.4 billion), driven by defense programs.</Callout>

## What happened
The ESA report highlights that European government space spending grew to 13.5 billion euros ($15.4 billion) in 2025, marking a 12% increase. This growth is significant as the global government space spending declined by 3% to 119 billion euros. The United States still leads with 58% of global government space budgets, followed by China at 15%, and Europe at 11%. Notably, Germany plans to invest 35 billion euros in space security and defense by 2030, while France is set to spend an additional 4.2 billion euros on military space activities between 2026 and 2040.

## Why it matters
The increase in defense spending is expected to have a positive impact on Europe's space economy. However, the report also notes that greater defense spending does not necessarily lead to lasting economic growth. The OECD and International Monetary Fund research suggests that long-term gains will depend on procurement strategies that foster innovation while containing costs, as well as cross-border coordination and harmonized standards.

## How to think about it
To understand the implications of increased defense spending on Europe's space economy, it's essential to consider the procurement strategies and cross-border coordination. Europe needs to foster innovation while containing costs and developing harmonized standards to unlock economies of scale.

<Callout type="warning">However, Europe's structural disadvantage in turning domestic demand into a larger, more cost-efficient industrial base compared to the United States and China remains a concern.</Callout>
