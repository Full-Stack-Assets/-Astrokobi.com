Japan’s space agency is gearing up to launch the Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission within months, targeting a historic landing on Phobos, one of Mars’ tiny moons. The spacecraft will spend roughly three years orbiting Mars, deploy the IDEFIX rover for surface studies, and collect at least 10 g of regolith before heading back to Earth. A return capsule is slated to touch down in Australia around 2031, offering the first pristine samples from a Martian moon. By probing Phobos’ composition, scientists hope to unlock clues about how the inner planets acquired water and organic material.

<Callout type="takeaway">MMX will bring back the first samples from a Martian moon, potentially reshaping our view of solar‑system formation.</Callout>

## What happened
The MMX spacecraft, built by JAXA and launched from Tanegashima Space Center, will enter Mars orbit and remain there for about three years. During this time it will conduct detailed mapping of Phobos and Deimos, with the French‑named IDEFIX rover performing on‑site observations on Phobos.

After a careful descent, MMX will touch down on Phobos, drill into the surface, and gather a minimum of 10 g of material. The collected sample will be sealed in a return capsule that will depart Mars, travel back to Earth, and land in the Australian outback in roughly 2031.

The mission will also observe Deimos from orbit, though it will not attempt a landing, and it proceeds amid a competitive timeline with a Chinese mission aiming to return samples first.

## Why it matters
Determining whether Phobos and Deimos are captured asteroids or the debris of a giant impact on Mars addresses two competing theories of moon formation. The answer informs models of how water, volatiles, and organic compounds may have been delivered to the inner rocky planets, a key factor in the emergence of habitable environments. Sample‑return data will also validate remote‑sensing techniques used on other bodies and lay groundwork for future human missions to Mars.

## How to think about it
Treat the MMX mission as a stepping stone: follow its milestones to gauge the readiness of sample‑return techniques, consider how its findings could reshape theories of planetary water delivery, and use the data to refine risk assessments for eventual human exploration of Mars and its moons.
