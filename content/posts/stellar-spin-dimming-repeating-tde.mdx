Stars that swing too close to a supermassive black hole can survive the encounter, only to return for another close pass. These repeating partial tidal disruption events (rpTDEs) produce bright flares each time, but astronomers have noticed that the flashes grow dimmer with each orbit. A recent study from Syracuse University links this fading to the star’s original spin rate, offering a physical explanation for a long‑standing puzzle. Understanding the role of stellar spin helps refine models of black‑hole feeding and the life cycles of stars in galactic cores.

<Callout type="takeaway">A star’s rapid spin before its first encounter can cause the progressive dimming of flares in repeating partial tidal disruption events.</Callout>

## What happened
The research examined several rpTDEs captured by wide‑field time‑domain surveys, focusing on how the brightness of each flare changed over successive encounters. By incorporating stellar rotation into tidal‑disruption simulations, the team reproduced the observed trend of steadily fainter flashes. The models show that a fast‑spinning star is partially stripped of its outer layers during the first close approach, leaving a more compact core that yields less luminous emission on later passes.

## Why it matters
If spin drives the fading, rpTDE light curves become a diagnostic tool for measuring stellar rotation in environments where direct observation is impossible. This insight improves estimates of how much material black holes accrete from stars, influencing predictions of black‑hole growth and feedback in galaxies. It also clarifies why some galactic nuclei show repeated, weakening outbursts rather than a single, catastrophic flare.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a new rpTDE, consider the star’s possible rotation as a factor alongside its mass and encounter distance. Compare the flare’s luminosity decay across multiple passages; a systematic decline may hint at a high‑spin progenitor. Use this perspective to prioritize follow‑up observations with time‑domain telescopes, aiming to capture early‑stage flares that hold the strongest spin signatures.

<Callout type="warning">Current models assume idealized spin orientations; real stars may have more complex rotation profiles that could alter predictions.</Callout>
