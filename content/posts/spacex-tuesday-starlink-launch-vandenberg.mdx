A new batch of Starlink satellites is set to lift off from California tonight, marking another step in SpaceX’s effort to expand global broadband coverage. The Falcon 9 is slated for liftoff at 9:01 p.m. PDT (12:01 a.m. EDT / 0401 UTC) from Space Launch Complex 4 East at Vandenberg Space Force Base. The mission, designated Starlink 17‑50, will place 24 additional satellites into low‑Earth orbit, pushing the total constellation to roughly 11,000 units. Reusing booster B1097 for its 12th flight, SpaceX also aims to land the first stage on the droneship *Of Course I Still Love You*, targeting the 219th landing on that vessel.

<Callout type="takeaway">The launch will expand Starlink’s network while showcasing SpaceX’s continued booster reusability.</Callout>

## What happened
SpaceX announced that the Falcon 9 will carry the Starlink 17‑50 payload, a set of 24 broadband internet satellites, on a south‑southwesterly trajectory after liftoff. The launch window opens at 9:01 p.m. PDT, with live coverage beginning about half an hour before the rocket ignites. The mission uses booster B1097, which previously supported the Twilight, Transporter‑17, Sentinel‑6B, NROL‑172, and seven earlier Starlink batches.

Approximately eight minutes after liftoff, the booster will attempt a controlled descent to the Pacific‑based droneship *Of Course I Still Love You*. If successful, this will be the 219th landing on that ship and the 651st Falcon booster landing overall, underscoring SpaceX’s rapid turnaround capabilities.

## Why it matters
Adding 24 satellites not only boosts Starlink’s capacity for high‑speed internet but also brings the fleet closer to the 12,000‑satellite threshold envisioned for near‑global coverage. Each successful booster recovery reduces launch costs, making frequent deployments financially viable and accelerating the rollout of new features such as Direct‑to‑Cell connectivity. The mission also demonstrates the reliability of Vandenberg’s south‑bound launch corridor for polar and sun‑synchronous orbits, a critical pathway for Earth‑observation and communications constellations.

## How to think about it
When evaluating the impact of a Starlink launch, consider three dimensions: coverage, cost, and sustainability. Coverage looks at how many new users can receive service and which regions gain connectivity. Cost examines how booster reuse and launch cadence affect pricing for end‑users and partners. Sustainability assesses orbital debris mitigation, end‑of‑life deorbit plans, and the broader implications for the low‑Earth‑orbit environment. By weighing these factors, observers can gauge whether a particular launch advances both commercial goals and responsible space stewardship.
