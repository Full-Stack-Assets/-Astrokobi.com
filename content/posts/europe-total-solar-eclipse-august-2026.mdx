On August 12, 2026, the Moon’s shadow will sweep across Europe, delivering the continent’s first total solar eclipse since the historic 1999 event. The path of totality will begin over the Arctic, cross Greenland and western Iceland, and then plunge over Spain and the Balearic Islands as evening approaches. NASA’s high‑resolution coronagraph aboard the Solar Dynamics Observatory is being positioned to capture unprecedented images of the Sun’s outer atmosphere during totality. Astronomers and casual sky‑watchers alike are gearing up for a rare blend of darkness, a bright corona, and even the Perseid meteor shower later that night.

<Callout type="takeaway">The August 12 eclipse offers Europe a once‑in‑a‑generation chance to see the Sun’s corona up close, with NASA providing the most detailed observations yet.</Callout>

## What happened
The total solar eclipse on August 12, 2026 will be the first to pass over mainland Europe since the August 1999 event, creating a darkened sky for up to two minutes where the Moon completely covers the Sun. The eclipse’s central line will travel from the high Arctic, through Greenland and western Iceland, before reaching the Iberian Peninsula, with Spain’s northern coast and the Balearic Islands experiencing the longest duration of totality.

NASA’s Solar Dynamics Observatory will employ its specialized coronagraph to record the Sun’s corona at wavelengths never before observed during a ground‑based total eclipse. Scientists aim to map the magnetic field structures that drive solar storms and to improve space‑weather forecasting models.

Because the eclipse occurs during the peak of the Perseid meteor shower, observers can expect a spectacular sky show: a darkened Sun, a glowing corona, and a rain of meteors streaking across the twilight horizon.

## Why it matters
Beyond the visual wonder, the event provides a unique laboratory for solar physics. Direct observations of the corona during totality can validate satellite data and refine our understanding of solar wind acceleration. Public interest spikes during eclipses, offering outreach opportunities that inspire the next generation of scientists. Economically, regions within the path of totality anticipate a tourism boom, with hotels and transport services preparing for an influx of visitors.

## How to think about it
Treat the eclipse as both a scientific experiment and a travel event. First, verify the local weather forecast for clear skies and identify the exact time of totality for your chosen site. Second, acquire ISO‑certified solar eclipse glasses and never look at the Sun without them, even during partial phases. Third, consider supplementing personal viewing with NASA’s live broadcast, which will stream the coronagraph footage in real time. Finally, plan for the Perseids by finding a dark‑sky location where you can enjoy both phenomena without light pollution.

<Callout type="warning">Never look directly at the Sun without proper solar‑filter glasses; doing so can cause permanent retinal damage.</Callout>
