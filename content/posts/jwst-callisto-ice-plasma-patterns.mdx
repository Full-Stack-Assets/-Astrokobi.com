The quiet, crater‑scarred moon Callisto has just revealed a surprising level of activity. Using JWST’s Near‑Infrared Spectrograph, an international team mapped water‑ice signatures across its surface with unprecedented detail. The new ice distribution maps expose distinct hemispheric patterns and a plasma‑shaped “bullseye” that reshape our view of the moon’s geologic and space‑weather history. These findings also provide a fresh comparative framework for the other Galilean moons and for icy bodies throughout the solar system.

<Callout type="takeaway">JWST shows that Callisto’s surface ice is actively modified by impacts and Jupiter’s magnetospheric plasma.</Callout>

## What happened
The team employed JWST’s NIRSpec to observe three key views of Callisto: the trailing hemisphere, the giant Asgard impact basin, and a centered view of Valhalla, the Solar System’s largest multi‑ring impact structure. The spectrograph detected the 3.1 µm “Fresnel peak,” a hallmark of crystalline water ice, and produced the most detailed ice‑distribution map of the moon to date.

On the leading hemisphere, the water‑ice signal tracks geographic features. Bright impact basins such as Valhalla and Asgard, along with younger craters like Lofn and Heimdall, exhibit sharp spikes in ice abundance, indicating that ancient impacts excavated fresher, brighter material that stands out against Callisto’s generally dark terrain. By contrast, the trailing hemisphere displays a distinct “bullseye” pattern: the ice signature is weakest near the equatorial center and strengthens toward higher latitudes, a distribution most plausibly shaped by plasma bombardment from Jupiter’s magnetosphere sweeping over the moon’s night side.

A secondary spectral feature near 4.25 µm was also identified, hinting at additional surface constituents that merit further investigation.

## Why it matters
The observations overturn the long‑standing view of Callisto as a geologically dead world. By linking ice distribution to both impact excavation and magnetospheric plasma, the study highlights how external forces continue to remodel icy surfaces even in the outer Solar System. This has direct implications for interpreting the histories of other icy moons, for refining models of surface‑space‑weather interactions, and for planning future missions—such as Europa Clipper and JUICE—that will encounter similar environments.

## How to think about it
When evaluating icy bodies, treat surface ice maps as a combination of geological exposure and external processing. Use the hemispheric contrast on Callisto as a template: bright impact sites likely expose fresher ice, while systematic patterns (e.g., the bullseye) can signal magnetospheric influence. Incorporate these dual drivers into models of surface evolution, and prioritize regions with anomalous ice signatures for future high‑resolution imaging or lander missions.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the observed ice signatures represent pure water ice; they may include mixtures or grain‑size effects that alter the spectral profile.</Callout>
