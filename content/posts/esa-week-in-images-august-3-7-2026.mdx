The European Space Agency’s weekly image roundup this week spotlights two striking stories. Sentinel‑2 satellite photos reveal the Danube north of Budapest at a record‑low level, a stark reminder of the severe drought gripping central Europe. At the same time, ESA offers a behind‑the‑scenes glimpse of the Orion spacecraft being prepared for NASA’s Artemis III mission inside a pristine cleanroom at Kennedy Space Center. Together these images illustrate how Earth‑observation and deep‑space exploration intersect in a world facing climate stress.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Danube’s record low and Orion’s cleanroom preparation underscore how satellite data and engineering vigilance are essential for both planetary health and human spaceflight.</Callout>

## What happened
Sentinel‑2’s high‑resolution optical sensors captured the Danube’s surface north of Budapest, showing water levels that have fallen to a historic minimum for the region. The imagery, part of ESA’s regular monitoring, highlights the intensity of the current drought, which has reduced river flow and exposed riverbanks that are normally submerged.

In a separate feature, ESA’s visual archive includes footage of the Orion crew capsule inside the Kennedy Space Center cleanroom. Technicians are inspecting every hinge, bolt, and surface to meet the stringent contamination standards required for Artemis III, the mission that will return humans to the Moon.

The ESA release also explains the “absolute severity” metric, a tool used to quantify loss of above‑ground biomass after disturbances such as drought, fire, or insect damage. This metric helps scientists assess ecosystem impacts across Europe.

## Why it matters
The Danube’s low water level is more than a striking photograph; it signals water‑stress for millions who rely on the river for drinking water, agriculture, and hydro‑electric power. Prolonged drought can exacerbate heatwaves, reduce crop yields, and increase the risk of wildfires across the basin.

Meanwhile, the meticulous preparation of Orion reflects the growing complexity of lunar missions. Cleanroom inspections are critical to prevent contamination that could jeopardize scientific experiments on the Moon’s surface and the health of the crew.
