NASA’s Learning Resources just released a new Career Spotlight that shines a light on mechanics for students aged 14‑18. The feature outlines the day‑to‑day work of aerospace, heavy‑equipment, and automotive mechanics across NASA centers such as Armstrong, Kennedy, and Michoud. It also maps out the education and certification routes teens can begin right after high school. Understanding these pathways matters because skilled mechanics keep the rockets, aircraft, and transport vessels that power America’s space program running safely.

<Callout type="takeaway">Mechanics are the unsung engineers who keep NASA’s hardware moving, and teens can start training now.</Callout>

## What happened
At NASA’s Armstrong Flight Research Center in Edwards, California, aerospace mechanics maintain research planes that test new wing designs and study atmospheric conditions. Over at Kennedy Space Center in Florida, mechanics service the massive crawler‑transporters that carry launch vehicles from the Vehicle Assembly Building to the launch pad. Meanwhile, at the Michoud Assembly Facility in New Orleans, mechanics keep a fleet of barges in top condition, ensuring rocket stages can be moved safely between sites.

Mechanics at NASA come from several trade backgrounds. Automotive mechanics focus on cars and trucks, often entering the field after a high‑school diploma and a technical‑school program. Heavy‑equipment mechanics typically complete a two‑year associate degree or apprenticeship, learning to service large construction and transport machines. NASA also employs specialized aerospace mechanics who receive on‑the‑job training and certifications specific to aircraft and launch‑vehicle hardware.

The agency provides clear pathways for teens: enroll in a high‑school vocational program, pursue certifications such as ASE for automotive work, or attend a community‑college associate program for heavy equipment. Early exposure through internships, summer workshops, or volunteering at local aerospace museums can also build relevant experience.

## Why it matters
Mechanics are the backbone of every launch and test flight; without reliable maintenance, missions face delays, cost overruns, or safety hazards. By highlighting these careers early, NASA helps address a growing skills gap in the aerospace sector and encourages a diverse pipeline of young talent. For students, the mechanic track offers a tangible, hands‑on way to contribute to space exploration without requiring a four‑year degree, expanding access to high‑impact STEM jobs.

## How to think about it
Start by researching local trade schools or community‑college programs that offer automotive or heavy‑equipment certifications. Pair classroom learning with hands‑on experience through part‑time jobs, school workshops, or youth robotics clubs that involve mechanical assemblies. Build a portfolio of completed projects—restored engines, repaired tools, or documented maintenance logs—to demonstrate competence. When you’re ready, look for NASA‑affiliated internships or summer programs that provide exposure to aerospace hardware. Finally, keep certifications current and consider joining professional societies such as the Society of Automotive Engineers to stay connected to industry standards.

<Callout type="warning">Never attempt to service NASA equipment or launch‑vehicle components without formal training, proper clearance, and safety supervision.</Callout>
