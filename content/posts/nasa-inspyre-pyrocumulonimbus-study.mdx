As wildfires intensify across the western United States, a new kind of storm is forming above the flames: the pyrocumulonimbus, a fire‑driven thunderhead that can loft smoke into the stratosphere. On August 3, NASA’s INSPYRE team dispatched a Gulfstream V aircraft into the plume of Utah’s Widemouth 2 fire, reaching roughly 8 miles (12 km) altitude to sample the cloud directly. The mission also employs the high‑altitude ER‑2 platform to complement the data set. By flying into these extreme clouds, scientists aim to uncover the mechanisms that turn a wildfire into a weather system. The findings could reshape how forecasters predict fire‑related storms and protect communities.

<Callout type="takeaway">Airborne sampling of pyrocumulonimbus clouds gives the only high‑resolution data needed to improve wildfire storm forecasts.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA announced on August 17 that the INSPYRE (Investigating the Nature of Pyro‑Cumulonimbus and its Radiative Effects) campaign would use two research aircraft this summer to fly directly into fire‑generated thunderstorms. The NSF/NCAR‑operated Gulfstream V and NASA’s high‑altitude ER‑2 were equipped with spectrometers, cloud‑physics probes, and lightning detectors to measure temperature, humidity, aerosol composition, and electrical activity inside the plume.

On August 3 the Gulfstream V entered the smoke plume from the Widemouth 2 fire in Utah, reaching an altitude of about 8 miles (12 km). Instruments recorded the vertical distribution of ash, black carbon, and water vapor, as well as the frequency of lightning strikes that accompany these clouds. The ER‑2 conducted complementary flights at even higher altitudes to track how plume particles disperse into the lower stratosphere.

The team hopes the in‑situ data will reveal why some intense fires generate pyrocumulonimbus clouds while others do not, and which vegetation types or terrain conditions most readily produce the thunderstorm‑like updrafts.

## Why it matters
Understanding the formation and evolution of pyrocumulonimbus clouds is critical for improving fire‑weather forecasts. These clouds can inject smoke and aerosols into the stratosphere, where they persist for months and affect regional climate and air‑quality models. Accurate measurements of their electrical activity also help predict lightning‑ignited spot fires that can dramatically expand a wildfire's footprint. By feeding real‑time aircraft observations into numerical weather prediction models, forecasters can issue more precise evacuation notices and allocate firefighting resources more effectively.

## How to think about it
Policymakers should treat pyrocumulonimbus formation as a measurable hazard rather than an anecdotal curiosity. Incorporating airborne data into regional fire‑weather suites can refine the timing and intensity of thunderstorm forecasts, which in turn guides evacuation timing and the deployment of aerial suppression assets. For communities living near fire‑prone forests, monitoring the potential for these storm‑like clouds should become part of standard wildfire risk assessments, especially during periods of extreme heat and low humidity.

<Callout type="warning">Data from a single flight cannot capture the full diversity of pyrocumulonimbus events, so model updates should be applied cautiously.</Callout>
