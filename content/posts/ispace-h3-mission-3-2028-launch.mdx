Japan’s burgeoning private space sector took a major step on July 28, when ispace and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries signed a launch agreement for the company’s third lunar mission. The deal designates Japan’s new H3 rocket as the launch vehicle for ispace’s Mission 3, slated for 2028, and will carry the company’s next‑generation Ultra lander to the Moon. This partnership aims to create Japan’s first privately led lunar transportation system, shifting reliance away from foreign launch services. If successful, it could reshape the economics of Japanese lunar exploration.

<Callout type="takeaway">Mission 3 will be the first Japanese private lunar payload launched on the H3, marking a new era of domestic lunar transport.</Callout>

## What happened
At a briefing on July 28, executives from ispace and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries signed an agreement that commits the H3 rocket to launch ispace’s Mission 3 in 2028. Mission 3 will be the inaugural flight of ispace’s Ultra lander, a design unveiled earlier this year, and the first time an H3 will carry a payload from a Japanese startup. The earlier two ispace landers flew on SpaceX Falcon 9 rockets in December 2022 and January 2025.

The choice of H3 was driven by several factors beyond national pride. ispace highlighted the vehicle’s payload capacity, launch environment, and “loading flexibility” as key advantages for accommodating the lander and its landing legs. The partnership also reflects a strategic response to the weakening yen, which makes dollar‑denominated launch services like Falcon 9 increasingly costly for Japanese companies. However, the H3’s track record raises concerns: out of eight launches, two have failed, including a December 2025 mission, though the rocket returned to flight with a test launch last month.

## Why it matters
By securing a fully domestic launch solution, Japan moves toward greater autonomy in lunar exploration and builds an ecosystem that could support future commercial and scientific missions. The agreement signals confidence in Japan’s emerging launch capabilities and may encourage other private firms to consider domestic options, potentially diversifying the global lunar market. At the same time, the H3’s mixed reliability record introduces risk, and the success of Mission 3 will be a litmus test for the viability of a Japanese private lunar transportation system.

## How to think about it
Stakeholders should monitor the H3’s upcoming test flights and any post‑flight analyses to gauge improvements in reliability. Companies considering Japanese launch services ought to factor in currency risk and potential schedule flexibility when budgeting. For policymakers, supporting a robust certification process and encouraging transparent cost reporting will help solidify confidence in the emerging domestic lunar transport infrastructure.

<Callout type="warning">The H3’s two past failures mean mission planners must build contingency timelines and consider backup launch options to mitigate schedule risk.</Callout>
