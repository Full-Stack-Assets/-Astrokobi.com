A powerful derecho swept across the Chicago metropolitan area last week, ripping trees and knocking down power lines across northwest Indiana. Satellite images taken by NASA before and after the storm show a stark contrast, with large swaths of darkness visible from orbit. The outage affected more than 60 % of NIPSCO’s customers, making it one of the utility’s largest events on record. Understanding the scale of the blackout matters for both emergency responders and policymakers. The visual evidence from space underscores how vulnerable regional grids can be to extreme weather.

<Callout type="takeaway">The derecho‑driven outage was so extensive that NASA’s satellites captured a night‑time blackout visible from space.</Callout>

## What happened

NASA’s Earth‑observing satellites recorded the night‑time lights over the Chicago‑northwest Indiana corridor on August 9, showing a bright, continuous glow. Four days later, on August 13, the same view revealed a darkened patch stretching across much of northwest Indiana, corresponding to the area hit by the derecho.

NIPSCO reported roughly 317,000 outages across the region, with more than 60 % of its customers without power at the peak of the event. Crews have restored service to over 163,000 customers, but as of 11 a.m. on Saturday, about 158,000 customers remained in the dark.

## Why it matters

The images illustrate how extreme weather can cripple a densely populated grid, exposing millions to prolonged power loss. Satellite monitoring provides an independent, real‑time assessment tool for utilities, emergency managers, and the public, helping to prioritize resources and communicate risk. The event also raises questions about grid hardening, vegetation management, and the role of climate‑driven storms in future infrastructure planning.

## How to think about it

When evaluating power‑outage risk, combine satellite observations with local outage reports to get a fuller picture. Communities should prioritize backup power for essential services and develop clear communication plans that reference both ground and space‑based data. Policymakers can use these visualizations to justify investments in grid hardening, such as underground lines and advanced weather‑forecasting integration.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume satellite darkness means all customers are without power; some areas may have localized restoration that is not yet reflected in orbital imagery.</Callout>
