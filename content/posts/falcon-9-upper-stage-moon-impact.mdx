Earlier this month a discarded SpaceX Falcon 9 upper stage slammed into the Moon at roughly 8,700 km/h. The stage had been adrift for 19 months after delivering the Firefly Blue Ghost and ispace Resilience landers in January 2025. It impacted an empty plain about 900 km from any other hardware, causing no immediate damage. The event arrives as NASA and China prepare crewed landings by 2030, raising concerns about uncontrolled debris on the lunar surface. Without clear disposal rules, future impacts could threaten both robotic and human missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">Uncontrolled lunar impacts expose a critical gap in international disposal guidelines.</Callout>

## What happened
The Falcon 9 upper stage, having exhausted its fuel, continued on a lunar‑bound trajectory until it struck the surface at a speed of approximately 8,700 km/h. The impact site was an uninhabited stretch roughly 900 km (550 mi) from any existing installations, so the collision produced no immediate operational loss.

This crash marks the second recent uncontrolled upper‑stage impact on the Moon. Current international rules, limited to the COSPAR planetary‑protection guidelines, address biological contamination but not the safety hazards of uncontrolled landings, debris ejection, or hazardous material release. The incident has revived discussion of lunar disposal standards, referenced in Artemis Accords Principle 12 and examined in a UK Space Agency analysis of post‑mission disposal options.

## Why it matters
As crewed missions target 2030, the lunar environment will host a mix of robotic assets, habitats, and infrastructure. Uncontrolled impacts can damage delicate equipment, create hazardous debris fields, and complicate navigation for future landers. Moreover, debris ejected during an impact can enter cislunar orbit, posing collision risks to satellites and spacecraft. The lack of binding disposal guidelines means each mission must assess risk in isolation, increasing the chance of costly accidents.

## How to think about it
Space agencies should incorporate semi‑controlled impact trajectories into mission designs, ensuring any final descent occurs in pre‑designated safe zones. Continuous tracking of upper stages after fuel depletion can improve situational awareness. Coordination through the Artemis Accords and national regulatory bodies can establish shared safety standards, while risk assessments must factor in both surface and orbital debris scenarios.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that any lunar region is free from impact risk; always verify the presence of uncontrolled debris before planning landings.</Callout>
