The evening sky takes a dramatic turn in August 2026, with Venus reaching its greatest eastern elongation on August 15 and sinking lower toward the west‑southwest horizon. At the same time, a tight Mercury‑Jupiter dawn conjunction offers a rare binocular spectacle just before sunrise. Saturn climbs high in the south‑southeast for prime telescopic viewing, while Mars slowly brightens as Earth closes the distance. These shifts are driven by the planets’ changing declinations and the tilt of the ecliptic as summer fades. Knowing the dates and positions lets both casual observers and seasoned amateurs make the most of the month’s celestial show.

<Callout type="takeaway">August delivers a packed lineup of planet pairings, with Venus at greatest elongation and a Mercury‑Jupiter conjunction to chase at dawn.</Callout>

## What happened
Venus reaches its greatest eastern elongation of about 46° on the evening of August 15, placing it low in the west‑southwest as its declination moves further south. This means the planet will set right at the end of evening twilight, disappearing into a brighter sky rather than a dark one.

Around the same time, Mercury and Jupiter appear together in a dawn conjunction. On August 15 they will be less than 0.6° apart, roughly the width of a clenched fist held at arm’s length, and will rise about an hour before sunrise in the east‑northeast.

Saturn offers the best telescopic target for the month, rising a few hours after sunset near the Cetus‑Pisces border and showing its iconic rings at a favorable tilt. Mars, still 172.2 million miles away at month’s end, brightens slowly as Earth approaches at roughly 425,800 mi (685,100 km) per day.

## Why it matters
These planetary motions illustrate how Earth’s orbit and the tilt of the ecliptic shape our night‑sky perspective. Venus’s low setting signals the transition toward autumn, while the Mercury‑Jupiter pairing provides a fleeting chance to compare two very different worlds side by side. For observers, the timing dictates when binoculars or a modest telescope will yield the richest views, and it highlights the importance of planning around twilight light levels.

## How to think about it
Start by checking your local sunset and sunrise times. For Venus, aim to look just after sunset on the western horizon; a clear view of the horizon will reveal the planet as a bright “evening star.” For the Mercury‑Jupiter conjunction, set up binoculars about 30 minutes before sunrise and point east‑northeast; use a hand‑fist measure to gauge their tight separation. Saturn is best observed with a small telescope after dark; locate the constellation Cetus and follow the ecliptic upward. Finally, keep an eye on Mars in the pre‑dawn sky, noting its gradual brightening over the month.

<Callout type="warning">Never look directly at the Sun or at the brightening twilight sky without proper solar filters; use binoculars only after the Sun is fully below the horizon.</Callout>
