Extreme heat, drought, and wildfires are scorching Europe in 2026, and ESA’s Earth‑observation satellites are capturing the crisis from orbit. June 2026 was the second‑warmest June on record globally, while Western Europe logged its hottest June ever. The combination of record temperatures, scarce rainfall, and strong winds has ignited widespread fires across Spain, France, Greece and beyond. Satellite data from Sentinel‑2, Sentinel‑3 and Sentinel‑5P reveal shrinking river levels, soaring sea‑surface temperatures, and thick smoke plumes. Understanding these patterns is essential for climate response and disaster management.

<Callout type="takeaway">Satellite monitoring links extreme heat, drought, and wildfire activity, highlighting climate‑driven risks across Europe.</Callout>

## What happened
ESA’s Copernicus Sentinel missions have been imaging the continent throughout July 2026. Sentinel‑2’s near‑infrared data show the Danube near Budapest at a record low, reflecting severe drought in central Europe. Sentinel‑5P captured a sharp rise in aerosol index over Spain and France as wildfires spread under dry conditions.

Sentinel‑3 observed a sea‑surface temperature anomaly in the Mediterranean on 29 June, confirming the heatwave’s reach over water. Meanwhile, false‑colour images from Sentinel‑2 illustrate dense smoke plumes from Greek and Spanish fires, and carbon‑monoxide concentrations over North America, underscoring the global nature of the phenomenon.

## Why it matters
The intertwined nature of heat, drought and fire amplifies greenhouse‑gas emissions, with up to 35 % of emissions originating from burning vegetation. Persistent dry conditions also reduce river flow, threatening water supplies and agriculture. Accurate, near‑real‑time satellite observations enable authorities to allocate resources, issue warnings, and model future climate impacts.

## How to think about it
Policymakers should integrate satellite alerts with ground‑based observations to prioritize evacuation zones and allocate firefighting assets. Researchers can combine temperature, moisture, and aerosol data to improve climate models that predict future heatwave frequency. Citizens can use publicly available ESA imagery to stay informed about local air quality and water‑resource conditions.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely solely on satellite imagery for fire‑risk decisions; always corroborate with on‑the‑ground reports and weather forecasts.</Callout>
