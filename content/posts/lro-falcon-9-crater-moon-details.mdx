On Aug. 5, 2026 a SpaceX Falcon 9 upper stage slammed into the lunar surface after its January 2025 launch of the Firefly Blue Ghost 1 mission. Six days later, NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter swung into position and captured a series of high‑resolution images of the fresh crater. The data reveal a 60‑foot‑wide rim, a shallow depth of less than 10 feet, and bright‑and‑dark rays that expose freshly excavated material. Understanding these impact signatures helps refine lunar hazard models and tests the precision of Earth‑based trajectory predictions.

<Callout type="takeaway">The LRO images confirm that a Falcon 9 upper‑stage created a 60‑foot crater with fresh ejecta, demonstrating the agency’s ability to locate and study artificial impacts on the Moon.</Callout>

## What happened
Between Aug. 11 and 12, 2026 the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) was tilted so its Narrow‑Angle Camera could point at the newly formed crater each time the spacecraft passed about 60 miles above the Moon, traveling roughly 1 mile per second. Because the Moon rotates beneath the orbiter, the team had to wait six days for the impact site to rotate into view, then time each exposure within a 10‑second window to keep the target centered.

The camera, capable of resolving features as small as 3 feet, captured the crater from multiple lighting angles. Scientists measured a rim width of about 60 feet and, based on shadow length, determined the crater is less than 10 feet deep. The impact excavated material from roughly 1.5 feet beneath the surface, producing bright rays of fresh rock near the rim and darker, weathered streaks farther out.

These observations were made possible by a global effort that combined trajectory data from NASA’s Center for Near‑Earth Object Studies, input from amateur astronomers, and coordination with the Korea Pathfinder Luna mission, which received the refined impact coordinates for follow‑up studies.

## Why it matters
Artificial impacts provide a controlled experiment for probing lunar regolith properties, helping scientists calibrate models of crater formation and ejecta dispersion. The precise location and timing also serve as a real‑world test of impact‑prediction tools that are critical for planetary defense and future lunar lander safety assessments. By comparing fresh‑impact signatures with those of ancient craters, researchers can better distinguish between natural and human‑made features on the Moon’s surface.

## How to think about it
Treat the Falcon 9 crater as a benchmark case: start with the known impact energy, compare the measured rim size and ejecta patterns, and then adjust regolith models accordingly. Use the timing precision required for LRO imaging as a reminder that future missions will need similarly exact pointing to monitor transient events. Finally, incorporate the collaborative workflow—trajectory refinement, amateur observations, and international data sharing—into planning for any planned impact experiments on the Moon or other bodies.

<Callout type="warning">Depth and volume estimates are based on shadow length and may vary by several feet; they should not be used as exact measurements for engineering designs.</Callout>
