The Perseid meteor shower, one of the year’s most prolific displays, is blazing across the sky right now. Its peak activity is forecast for the night of Aug 12 into Aug 13, when the Moon is new and the heavens are at their darkest. During the peak, observers can see up to 100 meteors per hour under ideal conditions. Knowing when and how to watch turns this fleeting spectacle into a memorable night under the stars.

<Callout type="takeaway">The peak of the Perseids on Aug 12‑13 offers the darkest, most meteor‑rich night of the season—plan a dark‑sky spot and look northeast.</Callout>

## What happened
The Perseids occur each year from mid‑July through late August as Earth sweeps through debris left by comet Swift‑Tuttle. The tiny particles burn up in the atmosphere, creating the familiar “shooting stars.” NASA describes the meteors as swift and bright, often leaving long wakes of light.

At the shower’s apex on Aug 12‑13, the combination of a new Moon and the Earth’s position maximizes visibility, allowing up to 100 meteors per hour for observers in dark locations. The best viewing direction is toward the northeast, where the constellation Perseus rises, providing the highest concentration of meteors.

## Why it matters
Seeing the Perseids connects casual skywatchers with the dynamic processes of our solar system, sparking curiosity about comets and orbital mechanics. Public viewing events also promote STEM engagement and remind us of Earth’s place in a broader cosmic environment. For photographers and hobbyists, the shower offers a rare chance to capture bright, fast meteors against a deep night sky.

## How to think about it
First, choose a viewing site far from streetlights—rural fields, parks, or high‑altitude spots work best. Check the weather forecast and aim for a night with no cloud cover. Arrive before total darkness and give your eyes roughly half an hour to adjust; avoid looking at phones or flashlights. Face northeast, locate Perseus, and relax, scanning the sky for brief streaks. Bring a reclining chair, warm clothing, and perhaps a hot drink, but keep electronic screens dim.

<Callout type="warning">Do not stare at bright lights or use binoculars to chase meteors, as this can damage your eyes and distract from the naked‑eye experience.</Callout>
