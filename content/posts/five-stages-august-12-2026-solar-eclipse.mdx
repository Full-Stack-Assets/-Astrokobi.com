The August 12, 2026 total solar eclipse is just days away, and millions are already marking their calendars. As the Moon slips between Earth and the Sun, a 182‑mile‑wide path of totality will sweep across Spain, Iceland and Greenland. For those outside the narrow corridor, a dramatic partial eclipse will still be visible across much of the globe. Understanding the five distinct stages helps viewers know what to look for and how to stay safe. Whether you’re traveling to the path or watching online, each phase offers a unique glimpse of celestial mechanics.

<Callout type="takeaway">The eclipse’s five stages guide both awe‑inspiring viewing and safe, scientific observation.</Callout>

## What happened
The first contact occurs at 11:34 a.m. EDT (1534 GMT) on August 12, when the Moon’s edge first kisses the Sun’s disk over Alaska, marking the start of the partial phase. Over the next hour and a half the Moon advances, carving a growing dark bite out of the Sun for observers in the path of totality.

Approximately thirty minutes before totality, the Sun shrinks to a thin crescent, the sky darkens noticeably, and temperatures begin to drop. This pre‑totality dimming signals the imminent onset of total darkness.

When the Moon completely covers the Sun’s photosphere, totality begins—a brief period when the solar corona becomes visible as a delicate, ethereal halo. After a few minutes, the Moon moves on, ending totality (third contact) and soon after the fourth contact restores the Sun’s full brilliance.

## Why it matters
Total solar eclipses provide rare opportunities to study the Sun’s corona, test general relativity, and engage the public in astronomy. The 2026 event’s wide path of totality promises significant scientific campaigns and a boost to tourism in the regions it traverses. For casual observers, the dramatic light changes and temperature dip create a memorable natural spectacle that deepens appreciation for orbital dynamics.

## How to think about it
Start by checking the exact timing for your location using an interactive eclipse tracker. If you plan to travel, book accommodations early, as demand spikes near the path. Always use ISO‑certified solar filters for any phase before totality; regular sunglasses are insufficient. Consider binoculars or a telescope with proper solar filters to enhance detail during the partial phases, and keep a pair of eclipse glasses handy for the brief moments of totality when it’s safe to look directly. Finally, stay flexible—weather can change quickly, so have a backup viewing spot if possible.

<Callout type="warning">Never look at the Sun without approved solar filters; doing so can cause permanent eye damage.</Callout>
