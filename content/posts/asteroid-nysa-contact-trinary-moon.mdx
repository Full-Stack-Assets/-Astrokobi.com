On July 29, 2026, astronomers using the Large Binocular Telescope in Arizona and the Very Large Telescope in Chile captured high‑resolution images of asteroid Nysa. The data reveal a remarkably irregular object that appears to be three lobes fused together, with a tiny satellite orbiting the system. If confirmed, Nysa would become the first known trinary asteroid, reshaping our view of how small bodies coalesce in the main belt.

<Callout type="takeaway">Nysa is likely a contact trinary asteroid with its own moon, a first for main‑belt objects.</Callout>

## What happened
Astronomers from Lowell Observatory reported that the new images show three distinct components connected by narrow neck‑like regions, a morphology they term "colli." The observations were published on arXiv on July 28, 2026 and have been accepted by *Astronomy & Astrophysics*.

In addition to the tri‑lobed shape, a faint point source was detected moving in concert with the main body, interpreted as a tiny moon. Nysa is classified as an E‑type asteroid, rich in enstatite, which gives it a high albedo and a visual magnitude around 6.75—bright enough for binoculars under dark skies.

## Why it matters
A confirmed contact trinary challenges existing models that primarily predict binary or single‑body asteroids in the belt. It suggests that low‑velocity collisions can produce more complex aggregates, offering a new laboratory for studying early solar‑system accretion. The presence of a moon also provides a natural probe of the system’s mass and density, informing impact‑risk assessments for similar objects.

## How to think about it
For observers, note that Nysa reaches magnitude ~6.75 near opposition, so a pair of 7×50 binoculars can reveal it as a faint star. Keep an eye on upcoming campaigns from both hemispheres; additional imaging will refine the lobes’ geometry and confirm the moon’s orbit. Researchers should treat Nysa as a case study when updating collisional evolution simulations of the main belt.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the moon’s orbit is stable enough for mission planning until further observations constrain its parameters.</Callout>
