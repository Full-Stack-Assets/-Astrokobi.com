When you watch footage of the International Space Station, the crew appears to float effortlessly, leading many to think that gravity no longer reaches them. In reality, Earth's gravity at the altitude of the ISS is only slightly weaker than at the surface, still about 90 % as strong. Astronauts experience weightlessness because the station and everything inside it are in a continuous state of free‑fall around Earth. This free‑fall creates a microgravity environment that profoundly affects daily life, science experiments, and future space missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">Weightlessness in orbit is caused by free‑fall, not the absence of gravity.</Callout>

## What happened
The International Space Station travels at roughly 7.66 km/s, matching the speed needed for a stable low‑Earth orbit. At this speed, the station is constantly falling toward Earth, but because Earth curves away beneath it, the fall never reaches the surface. This condition—continuous free‑fall—is what creates the sensation of weightlessness for everything aboard.

Because both the spacecraft and its occupants share the same acceleration toward Earth, there is no normal force pushing them against a floor. Without that contact force, our bodies register no weight, even though the gravitational pull remains strong.

## Why it matters
Microgravity enables experiments that cannot be performed on Earth, such as studying fluid behavior, crystal growth, and protein folding without the interference of buoyancy or sedimentation. For astronauts, however, prolonged weightlessness leads to muscle atrophy, bone density loss, and fluid redistribution, all of which must be mitigated for long‑duration missions.

Understanding that gravity is still present informs spacecraft design, life‑support planning, and the development of countermeasures like resistance exercise and artificial gravity concepts for future deep‑space travel.

## How to think about it
Treat an orbiting spacecraft as a constantly falling elevator. If you were inside an elevator whose cables were cut, you would feel weightless because both you and the cabin accelerate downward at the same rate. The same principle applies to the ISS: it is falling around Earth, not into it. When planning missions or interpreting data from space, remember that the apparent lack of weight is a result of shared free‑fall, not a disappearance of the gravitational force.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that processes behave exactly as they do on Earth; microgravity changes fluid dynamics, combustion, and material behavior, so ground‑based tests may not translate directly.</Callout>
