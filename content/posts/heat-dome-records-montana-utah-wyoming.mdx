On July 12, 2026, a massive heat dome settled over the northern Rockies, sending temperatures soaring past 45 °C (113 °F) in parts of Montana, Utah, and Wyoming. Satellite data combined with the GEOS model highlighted a swath of extreme heat that broke all-time records at multiple weather stations. The phenomenon was driven by a persistent ridge of high pressure that trapped warm air near the surface. Such unprecedented heat spikes raise concerns for public health, water resources, and future climate trends. Understanding the mechanics behind the dome helps scientists gauge how similar events may evolve as the planet warms.

<Callout type="takeaway">The July 2026 heat dome set new temperature records across the western U.S., illustrating how high‑pressure ridges can amplify extreme heat.</Callout>

## What happened
The ridge of high pressure that lingered over the northern Rockies on the weekend of July 11‑12, 2026 forced hot air downward, creating a classic heat dome that suppressed convection and cloud formation. This atmospheric setup allowed sunlight to reach the surface unimpeded, rapidly raising temperatures.

National Weather Service observations recorded all-time highs at several sites: Billings, MT hit 111 °F, Miles City, MT reached 115 °F (a 4 °F jump), and Sheridan, WY logged 109 °F. In Utah, Deseret recorded 111 °F, Salt Lake City 109 °F (4 °F above its previous record), and Randolph 100 °F (6 °F above its historic high). Each station surpassed records that dated back to the early 20th century.

The temperature map for July 12, 2026, at 2 p.m. Mountain Time was generated by blending satellite observations with the GEOS atmospheric model, showing the darkest reds where temperatures approached or exceeded 45 °C.

## Why it matters
Extreme heat directly threatens human health, especially older adults with cardiovascular, respiratory, or kidney conditions. The record‑breaking temperatures also strain power grids, increase wildfire risk, and stress water supplies in already arid regions. From a climate perspective, the event provides a concrete example of how persistent high‑pressure systems can intensify heat waves as the baseline climate warms.

## How to think about it
Treat heat‑dome events as a signal that extreme temperature spikes are becoming more common. Communities should prioritize heat‑action plans, ensure vulnerable populations have access to cooling centers, and incorporate heat resilience into building codes. Individuals can protect themselves by staying hydrated, limiting outdoor activity during peak heat hours, and monitoring local health advisories.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a single record‑breaking day represents a permanent new norm; temperature records are regularly reviewed and may be adjusted as more data become available.</Callout>
