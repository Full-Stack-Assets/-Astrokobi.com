On 18 August, ESA astronaut Sophie Adenot will step outside the International Space Station for her first EVA, teaming with NASA astronaut Anil Menon. The pair will exit the Quest airlock around 12:35 GMT to replace a Space‑to‑Ground Antenna that is essential for high‑speed data transmission between Houston and the station. The six‑and‑a‑half‑hour spacewalk will be streamed live on ESA WebTV and YouTube, giving the public a front‑row seat to a critical maintenance operation. This mission marks the fifth French spacewalk and underscores the growing role of ESA astronauts in deep‑space operations.
<Callout type="takeaway">Sophie Adenot’s debut EVA demonstrates ESA’s expanding presence in ISS maintenance and offers a live glimpse of critical communications work.</Callout>
## What happened
Sophie Adenot and Anil Menon will launch from the Quest airlock at approximately 12:35 GMT/14:35 CEST, assisted by ISS crew members Jessica Meir and Jack Hathaway who will operate the Canadarm2 to position the astronauts. The EVA, scheduled for about six and a half hours, focuses on removing the aging Space‑to‑Ground Antenna (SGANT) and installing a new unit that restores high‑speed telemetry between the station and Mission Control in Houston.
Both astronauts have spent extensive time training underwater—Adenot more than 150 hours—to simulate microgravity and master the tools required for the antenna swap. NASA’s Anil Menon will wear the red‑striped EVA suit, while Adenot’s suit will be stripe‑free, reflecting ESA’s distinct design.
The live broadcast will be available on ESA WebTV and the agency’s YouTube channel, starting at 11:00 GMT/13:00 CEST, with commentary from mission specialists.
## Why it matters
Replacing the SGANT is vital for maintaining the bandwidth needed for scientific data, video downlinks, and crew communications; any failure could degrade mission operations. The successful participation of an ESA astronaut in a high‑profile EVA highlights Europe’s growing technical contribution to the ISS partnership and builds confidence for future deep‑space missions. Publicly streaming the EVA also strengthens outreach, allowing millions to witness the complexity and bravery of spacewalks in real time.
