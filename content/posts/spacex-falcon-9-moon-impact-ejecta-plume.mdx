Tonight a massive 45‑foot, 8,000‑pound Falcon 9 upper stage will slam into the Moon’s western limb near Einstein Crater. The stage, a leftover from the January 2025 launch of Firefly’s Blue Ghost and ispace’s Hakuto‑R missions, is slated to impact at 2:35 a.m. EDT (0635 UTC). While the impact flash will be lost against the sun‑lit surface, scientists and amateur astronomers are watching for a possible ejecta plume that could rise up to 100 km above the lunar horizon. If visible, the plume would offer a rare glimpse of a human‑made impact on another world and engage the public in lunar science.

## What happened
The discarded upper stage, measuring 13.8 meters in length, is traveling at roughly 5,400 mph (8,700 km/h) as it descends onto the Moon. Its trajectory will intersect a sunlit portion of the lunar surface, striking near the western limb where the Earth‑facing side meets the dark horizon. Project Pluto simulations suggest the impact could loft debris up to 100 kilometers, forming a faint, expanding plume against the blackness of space.

Visibility will be challenging. For observers on the U.S. East Coast the Moon will be only about 40 degrees above the southeastern horizon, giving a low viewing angle before dawn. Parts of South America will have a higher Moon and a more favorable line of sight. Advanced telescopes and high‑speed cameras will be needed to capture any fleeting plume, and even then detection is uncertain.

## Why it matters
An observable ejecta plume would provide real‑time data on impact physics in the lunar environment, helping refine models of how debris behaves on airless bodies. Public interest in the event could boost amateur participation in lunar observation and inspire future citizen‑science projects. Conversely, the difficulty of detection highlights the limits of ground‑based monitoring for small‑scale lunar impacts, underscoring the need for dedicated lunar surveillance assets.

## How to think about it
If you plan to watch, set up a telescope with a moderate focal length and attach a video camera capable of high frame rates. Aim for a clear, dark horizon and use a lunar filter to reduce glare. Record continuously for at least ten minutes before and after the predicted impact time, then review the footage frame by frame for any sudden brightening or expanding cloud. Share any credible detections with the lunar observation community so they can be cross‑checked.

<Callout type="warning">Do not expect a guaranteed visual flash; the plume may be too faint to detect without specialized equipment.</Callout>
