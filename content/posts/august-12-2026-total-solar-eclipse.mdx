The sky will darken on August 12, 2026 as the Moon slips directly in front of the Sun, producing a total solar eclipse visible from parts of Europe and the North Atlantic. Observers in Spain, Iceland, and Greenland will experience a brief but dramatic plunge into night, with the longest stretch of totality lasting 2 minutes and 18 seconds over the ocean. Because the eclipse coincides with the peak of the Perseid meteor shower, sky‑watchers may also catch a meteor streaking across the darkened horizon. This rare alignment offers both a scientific showcase and a spectacular photo opportunity for enthusiasts worldwide.

<Callout type="takeaway">The August 12 eclipse provides a short, high‑impact viewing window across Europe, enhanced by the Perseids meteor shower.</Callout>

## What happened
On Wednesday, August 12, 2026, the Moon will completely cover the Sun for viewers situated within the narrow path of totality that stretches from the western coast of Spain, across Iceland, and into Greenland. At its peak, near the centerline over the North Atlantic, totality will last 2 minutes and 18 seconds—shorter than the 2024 North American eclipse but still impressive.

In Spain, the eclipse will occur close to sunset, giving observers roughly 1 minute and 50 seconds of total darkness while the Sun sits only a few degrees above the western horizon. This low‑angle setting promises dramatic silhouettes of the landscape beneath the eclipsed Sun.

Because the Moon is new on the same day, the Perseid meteor shower will be at its annual maximum, raising the chance that a meteor will be visible during the few minutes of totality for anyone under clear skies.

## Why it matters
A total solar eclipse is a natural laboratory for studying the Sun’s corona, solar wind, and Earth’s ionosphere, all of which become accessible when the bright solar disk is blocked. The August 2026 event, though brief, will allow researchers in Europe to gather data that complement observations from the longer 2027 African eclipse. For the public, the eclipse’s timing with the Perseids adds a cultural and educational hook, encouraging wider participation in citizen‑science projects and boosting interest in astronomy.

## How to think about it
Plan your viewing site early, targeting locations within the path of totality that offer clear horizons—coastal Spain or open fields in Iceland are ideal. Secure ISO‑certified eclipse glasses well before the event; never look at the Sun without proper protection, even during partial phases. If you hope to capture the Perseids, bring a wide‑angle lens and be ready to switch focus quickly when totality begins. Finally, consider joining local astronomy clubs or online live‑stream groups to share observations and enhance safety.

<Callout type="warning">Never view the Sun without certified eclipse glasses during the partial phases, and never point telescopes or binoculars at the Sun without proper solar filters.</Callout>
