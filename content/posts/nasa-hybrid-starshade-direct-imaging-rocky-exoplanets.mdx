Astronomers have long struggled to capture the faint glow of Earth‑like worlds orbiting distant stars. A NASA‑led team now proposes a hybrid observatory that pairs an orbital starshade with the upcoming Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) in Chile. The starshade would sit about 175,000 km from Earth, casting an artificial eclipse that blocks starlight and lets the ELT record reflected light from planets up to 20 light‑years away. If successful, the concept could deliver the first direct images of rocky exoplanets in visible light. The approach promises a shortcut around the limitations of current space‑based coronagraphs.

<Callout type="takeaway">A space‑based starshade combined with a ground‑based ELT could directly image nearby rocky exoplanets.</Callout>

## What happened
The proposed Hybrid Observatory for Earth‑like Exoplanets (HOEE) would deploy a 50‑meter‑diameter starshade with 48 24.5‑meter petals into an elliptical orbit roughly 175,000 km from Earth. When positioned between a target star and the ELT, the starshade creates an artificial eclipse, suppressing starlight by a factor of about a billion and allowing the ELT to capture reflected optical light from Earth‑sized planets.

The team has submitted a Phase B proposal to NASA’s Innovative Advanced Concepts (NIAC) program, seeking funding by 2027. Current instruments such as JWST’s NIRCam and the Roman Space Telescope’s coronagraph cannot directly detect Earth‑like planets in reflected visible light, a limitation highlighted in a 2026 Nature Astronomy paper.

## Why it matters
Direct optical imaging of rocky worlds would open a new window on planetary atmospheres, surface conditions, and potential biosignatures, complementing transit spectroscopy. By leveraging the ELT’s massive collecting area, the hybrid system could achieve high‑resolution spectra without the expense of building a dedicated space telescope of comparable size. Success would accelerate the search for habitable environments and inform the design of future flagship missions.

## How to think about it
Researchers should prioritize nearby Sun‑like stars within the 20‑light‑year horizon and develop robust formation‑flying algorithms that can maintain the starshade’s position despite orbital perturbations. Parallel work on high‑contrast data processing will be essential to extract the faint planetary signal. Stakeholders can view the starshade‑ELT pairing as a stepping stone, informing the architecture of later, more ambitious space‑based direct‑imaging missions.

<Callout type="warning">The concept hinges on achieving unprecedented formation‑flying accuracy and securing sustained funding; premature investment without prototype validation could divert resources from other exoplanet programs.</Callout>
