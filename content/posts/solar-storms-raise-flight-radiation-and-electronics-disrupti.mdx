When a solar storm erupts, the invisible particles it hurls toward Earth can turn a routine transatlantic flight into a radiation hotspot. A new University of Surrey Space Centre study quantified the effect of a November 2025 storm, finding radiation levels at typical cruising altitudes rose to almost ten times the background. The same research compared that spike to the historic February 1956 event, which would have delivered a full year’s worth of flight‑related cosmic dose in a single journey. These findings matter because they expose both health risks for passengers and a hidden vulnerability in modern aircraft electronics.
<Callout type="takeaway">Extreme solar storms can dramatically increase in‑flight radiation and trigger thousands of electronic glitches.</Callout>
## What happened
The Surrey team measured a solar storm in November 2025 that briefly pushed radiation at 8‑14 km altitude to nearly ten times normal levels. By contrast, the February 1956 storm—a benchmark for extreme space weather—would have exposed a passenger to roughly a year’s worth of cumulative flight radiation in a single trip.
In addition to the dose spike, the study documented a surge in single‑event upsets (SEUs) within aircraft avionics. Where normal conditions produce a handful of SEUs per hour, the extreme event drove the rate into the thousands, overwhelming error‑correction systems. Airbus responded to a related incident last year by grounding 6,000 A320s after a JetBlue flight suffered a flight‑control computer failure traced to high‑energy particle intrusion.
## Why it matters
Radiation exposure at altitude is already higher than on the ground; a tenfold increase pushes doses into a range where short‑term health effects become a realistic concern for crew and passengers, especially on polar routes where the magnetic shielding is weakest. Simultaneously, the cascade of electronic glitches threatens flight‑control reliability, potentially leading to emergency landings or widespread fleet groundings, which carry economic and safety repercussions for airlines and regulators.

## How to think about it
Airlines should integrate real‑time space‑weather alerts into flight‑planning software, consider temporary altitude adjustments on polar legs, and prioritize retrofitting critical avionics with radiation‑tolerant components. Pilots and crew need clear protocols for elevated radiation events, and regulators ought to update dose‑limit guidelines to reflect these extreme scenarios.
<Callout type="warning">Current data are based on a limited number of extreme events; predictions remain uncertain, so operators should avoid over‑reliance on a single model when making safety decisions.</Callout>
