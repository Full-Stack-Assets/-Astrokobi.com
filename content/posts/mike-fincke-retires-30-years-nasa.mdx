After three decades of service, veteran NASA astronaut Mike Fincke announced his retirement on August 12, 2026. Fincke leaves the agency as its fourth‑most‑flown astronaut, tallying 549 days in orbit across four missions. His career spans two ISS commander tours, nine spacewalks, and a recent stint as pilot on SpaceX’s Crew‑11 flight. The announcement marks the end of an era for a figure who has helped shape modern human spaceflight. Understanding his legacy offers insight into the experience base that underpins current and future missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">Mike Fincke’s retirement ends a 30‑year career that helped define modern ISS operations.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA confirmed on Wednesday, Aug. 12, that Mike Fincke will retire after 30 years with the agency. The statement highlighted his status as the fourth‑most‑flown astronaut in NASA history, with a cumulative 549 days in space over four separate flights. Fincke’s most recent mission was as pilot of SpaceX’s Crew‑11, after which he served as commander of ISS Expedition 74 from August 2025 to January 2026.

Fincke’s résumé includes four launches, nine extravehicular activities, and two stints as ISS commander. He first flew to the station aboard Soyuz TMA‑4 in 2004 for Expedition 9, and later commanded the station during Expedition 57 and Expedition 74. His extensive EVA experience and leadership roles have been integral to ISS assembly and maintenance.

Before joining NASA’s 1996 astronaut class, Fincke earned dual bachelor’s degrees from MIT and completed master’s programs at Stanford and the University of Houston‑Clear Lake. He served as a test pilot in the U.S. Air Force, worked on the Japanese‑U.S. XF‑2 fighter program, and later acted as ISS CAPCOM and crew procedures lead in Russia.

## Why it matters
Fincke’s departure removes a deep well of operational knowledge from NASA’s astronaut corps, particularly in long‑duration missions and EVA execution. His experience informs crew training, mission planning, and international partnership protocols; losing that mentorship could affect the readiness of newer astronauts. At the same time, his retirement opens a senior slot for the next generation, signaling a shift toward newer talent that will shape the agency’s Artemis and lunar gateway ambitions.
