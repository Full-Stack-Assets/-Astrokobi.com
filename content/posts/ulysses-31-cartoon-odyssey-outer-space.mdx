The '80s were a time of great creativity in animation, and one series stands out for its unique blend of Greek mythology and science fiction: 'Ulysses 31'. This French-Japanese co-production brought Homer's classic tale, 'The Odyssey', to a futuristic setting, captivating a generation of young viewers.

<Callout type="takeaway">'Ulysses 31' is a space opera that reimagines Homer's 'The Odyssey' in a futuristic setting, following the adventures of Ulysses and his crew.</Callout>

## What happened
'Ulysses 31' follows the story of Ulysses, a space captain who must navigate the challenges of the galaxy while trying to return home to his wife, Penelope. His journey is filled with action, adventure, and encounters with mythological creatures like the Cyclops. The series takes creative liberties with Homer's original tale, incorporating elements of science fiction and fantasy.

The show's premise is centered around Ulysses' quest to rescue his son, Telemachus, who has been abducted by the Cyclops, and to outwit the gods, who are determined to thwart his return home. Along the way, he meets new allies, including Yumi and Numinor, a pair of blue alien Zotrians.

The series features a mix of science fiction and fantasy elements, with a unique blend of futuristic technology and mythological creatures. The show's visuals and storytelling were influenced by the era's popular sci-fi franchises, such as 'Star Wars'.

## Why it matters
'Ulysses 31' matters because it represents a unique fusion of science fiction and classical mythology, offering a fresh take on a timeless story. The series has become a cult classic, cherished by fans of animation and science fiction. Its influence can be seen in later works that have drawn inspiration from Homer's 'The Odyssey'.

The show's impact extends beyond its entertainment value, as it introduced a generation of viewers to the rich world of Greek mythology. By reimagining the classic tale in a futuristic setting, 'Ulysses 31' made the story more accessible and engaging for a new audience.
