NASA is preparing a crucial Earth‑orbit demonstration of its Human Landing System partners ahead of the Artemis III crewed lunar landing. In 2027 the agency will launch test articles of SpaceX’s Starship V3 and Blue Origin’s Blue Moon lander on separate rockets, allowing astronauts to practice rendezvous, docking, and crew transfer in low‑Earth orbit. The exercise is designed to validate flight software, life‑support hardware, and operational procedures that will later be used on the Moon. By rehearsing these steps now, NASA hopes to reduce risk for the 2028 Artemis IV mission that will carry the first crew back to the lunar surface since 1972.

<Callout type="takeaway">The 2027 Earth‑orbit test will prove the HLS docking and crew‑transfer sequence needed for Artemis III and IV.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA will conduct the demonstration in low‑Earth orbit using two commercial HLS test articles. SpaceX’s Starship V3 will launch on its own Super Heavy booster, while Blue Origin’s Blue Moon Mark 2 will lift off aboard a New Glenn vehicle. Once both landers reach orbit, an Orion crew capsule launched on the SLS will rendezvous with each vehicle in turn, allowing astronauts to practice the docking maneuvers and to enter the lander cabins.

The test articles carry full flight software, avionics, and an Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) identical to the crewed versions. Blue Origin’s lander also includes a lunar‑surface spacesuit mass simulator, mirroring the “Moonikin” payload used on Artemis I, to provide real‑time data on mass distribution and suit interface loads during crew transfer.

## Why it matters
Validating the docking and crew‑transfer workflow in Earth orbit removes a major unknown before the first crewed lunar landing. Successful data collection will inform software updates, hardware refinements, and crew‑training protocols, tightening the schedule for Artemis IV’s 2028 launch. The test also demonstrates the viability of two competing commercial providers, reinforcing NASA’s strategy of diversified partnerships that can lower costs and increase resilience.

## How to think about it
Treat the 2027 rehearsal as a milestone rather than a guarantee. Follow NASA’s post‑flight data releases to see which systems performed as expected and which require redesign. If you are tracking lunar‑economy opportunities, note that successful HLS validation could accelerate commercial payload deliveries to the Moon, opening new markets for in‑situ resource utilization. For educators, the test offers concrete examples of how software, life‑support, and human factors intersect in crewed exploration.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the Earth‑orbit test fully replicates lunar gravity, surface conditions, or radiation environment.</Callout>
