On the night of August 12, 2026, a high‑speed video captured over Spain showed a fleeting streak crossing a partially eclipsed Sun. The footage appeared in NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day for August 19, sparking headlines about a “maybe meteor.” Astronomers quickly examined the timing, location, and the Perseids meteor shower peak to determine the object's true nature. The investigation revealed that the streak was not a meteoroid at all but an aircraft contrail illuminated by the Sun. Understanding this misidentification helps both scientists and the public interpret transient sky events more accurately.

<Callout type="takeaway">The mysterious streak was an illuminated airplane contrail, not a Perseids meteor.</Callout>

## What happened
The 24‑frame‑per‑second video was recorded at 20:28 local time from coordinates 40°34'30.3"N 1°12'28.8"W in Spain, just as the Perseids meteor shower reached its peak. The bright, linear feature appeared to move across the Sun’s partially eclipsed disk, prompting speculation that it might be a meteor tracing back to the Perseids radiant in Perseus.

Closer analysis showed the object's apparent angular width was smaller than the 0.5° diameter of the Sun and Moon, inconsistent with the typical fireball trail of a meteor. Its brightness did not extend far beyond the solar limb, suggesting reflected sunlight rather than self‑emission. By cross‑referencing the time and location with commercial flight databases, researchers identified a high‑altitude aircraft whose contrail matched the observed geometry, confirming the source.

## Why it matters
The episode illustrates how bright aircraft can masquerade as meteors, especially during high‑profile showers when observers are primed to expect fireballs. Misidentifications can inflate meteor counts, skew statistical studies, and fuel public misconceptions about atmospheric phenomena. By documenting the investigative process, the APOD entry reinforces the need for rigorous cross‑checking of visual reports, supporting more reliable citizen‑science contributions.

## How to think about it
When you spot a brief, bright line near the Sun or Moon, first note the exact time and direction. Compare the apparent width to the known angular size of the Sun (≈0.5°). Check online flight‑tracker maps for aircraft at the corresponding altitude and bearing. If the streak aligns with a known flight path and its width is unusually narrow, an illuminated contrail is the more likely explanation. Using this simple checklist helps separate genuine meteors from atmospheric or human‑made artifacts.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume any brief bright streak near the Sun is a meteor without consulting flight data and angular measurements.</Callout>
