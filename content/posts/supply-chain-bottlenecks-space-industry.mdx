The United States launched more than 3,700 objects in 2025—almost ten times the 2019 level—showing how quickly the space economy is expanding. That rapid growth is colliding with a supply chain built for a slower, lower‑volume market. Manufacturers now report bottlenecks in optical links, propellant tanks, and radiation‑tolerant connectors, with as few as three domestic suppliers for some parts. The resulting lead‑time extensions are already lengthening program schedules and raising costs for both commercial and government missions. Understanding and fixing these constraints is essential to keep the sector’s momentum.

<Callout type="takeaway">Supply chain limits are the next bottleneck that could curb the space industry’s growth.</Callout>

## What happened
The industry’s launch cadence exploded in 2025, with more than 3,700 objects lifted off from U.S. soil—roughly ten times the 2019 figure. At the same time, the sector is shifting from bespoke, low‑volume production to industrial‑scale manufacturing, a transition highlighted in a recent PwC and Aerospace Industries Association report.

Manufacturers are already feeling strain in specialized components such as optical inter‑satellite links, propellant tanks, and radiation‑tolerant connectors, where the domestic market sometimes relies on as few as three suppliers. Over the past five years aerospace manufacturing output grew 30%, yet overall industry capacity utilization hovers around 74%, prompting firms to stretch existing facilities, extend lead times, and delay certification of flight‑ready hardware.

## Why it matters
When critical parts become scarce, launch schedules slip and mission costs rise, affecting everything from commercial broadband constellations to national‑security payloads. A constrained supply base also limits the United States’ ability to compete with emerging space powers that are building more diversified industrial ecosystems. The longer‑term risk is a self‑reinforcing slowdown: higher costs discourage investment, which in turn reduces the incentive to expand production capacity.

## How to think about it
Stakeholders should map critical components, identify where supplier concentration exceeds three sources, and prioritize funding for capacity expansion in those niches. Policy makers can incentivize dual‑use facilities that serve both defense and commercial markets, while companies can adopt modular designs that allow alternative parts to be qualified more quickly. Building a transparent, data‑driven supply‑chain dashboard can help monitor utilization rates and flag emerging shortages before they impact launch windows.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume existing suppliers can meet future demand without investing in new capacity.</Callout>
