Rocket Lab announced today that its new Global Hypersonic & Orbital Spaceport Technology—nicknamed GHOST—will let the company launch its Electron and HASTE rockets from virtually any location. The system fits inside standard shipping containers and includes rockets, launch pads, ground support and range‑control equipment. By turning a launch site into a deployable kit, Rocket Lab aims to run multi‑launch campaigns and support time‑critical national‑security missions without relying on fixed infrastructure. The move could reshape how small‑satellite operators schedule launches and how defense agencies test hypersonic technologies.

<Callout type="takeaway">GHOST makes launch infrastructure truly mobile, expanding Rocket Lab’s reach beyond its two permanent sites.</Callout>

## What happened
The portable spaceport, officially called GHOST, packs the complete Electron or HASTE launch stack into a set of ISO‑standard containers. Once the containers arrive on site, they can be assembled into a functional launch pad, complete with fuel handling, telemetry, and range‑control systems, allowing a launch within days.

Rocket Lab currently operates from Mahia Peninsula in New Zealand and Wallops Island, Virginia, and has announced a third site at Alaska’s Pacific Spaceport Complex. The company plans to deploy two GHOST pads at the Alaskan complex during 2026, extending its footprint to high‑latitude orbits and remote test ranges.

## Why it matters
A mobile launch capability reduces dependence on a limited number of fixed pads, which can be constrained by weather, scheduling conflicts, or geopolitical considerations. For small‑satellite customers, the ability to launch from a location that matches an optimal orbital inclination could lower fuel costs and shorten time‑to‑orbit. For national‑security missions, rapid deployment of a launch site enables testing of hypersonic technologies or responsive satellite launches in regions where traditional infrastructure is unavailable.

## How to think about it
Treat GHOST as a modular launch service rather than a single launch site. Evaluate potential launch locations based on orbital requirements, regulatory environment, and local support infrastructure. Incorporate the deployment timeline into mission planning—allow weeks for site clearance, container setup, and system checks before a launch window can be booked.

<Callout type="warning">Regulatory clearance and local environmental reviews can add weeks to a GHOST deployment, so customers should not assume an instant launch capability.</Callout>
