The night sky this August will host a special event: the Sturgeon Moon, the full moon that marks the height of the salmon‑run season, will slip into Earth’s shadow. As the Moon moves directly opposite the Sun, the planet blocks sunlight, casting a reddish hue on the lunar surface. This lunar eclipse will be visible across much of the Northern Hemisphere, offering a striking reminder of the Sun–Earth–Moon geometry that drives tides and calendars. For stargazers, the brief darkening provides a chance to see the Moon’s surface details in sharper contrast. Understanding why this happens deepens our appreciation of the dynamic Earth–Moon system.

## What happened
The full moon occurs when Earth is positioned between the Sun and the Moon, so the lunar near side is fully illuminated for observers on Earth. This alignment happens roughly once a month and defines the moment when the Sun‑Earth‑Moon angle reaches 180°.

When that alignment coincides with the Moon passing through Earth’s shadow, a lunar eclipse takes place. The Earth’s umbra—its central, darkest shadow—covers part or all of the Moon, dimming its brightness and often giving it a coppery hue as sunlight filters through the atmosphere.

In August 2026 the full moon, traditionally called the Sturgeon Moon, will intersect Earth’s umbra, creating a partial to total lunar eclipse that can be observed without special equipment.

## Why it matters
Lunar eclipses are more than visual spectacles; they illustrate the precise orbital mechanics that regulate the Earth‑Moon system and affect tidal patterns that have shaped human settlement. Culturally, the Sturgeon Moon has long signaled seasonal changes for fishing communities, and its eclipse adds a layer of mythic resonance. Scientifically, observing the eclipse can help amateur astronomers practice timing measurements and compare the Moon’s color changes to atmospheric conditions.

## How to think about it
First, check your local moonrise and moonset times for the night of the August full moon; the eclipse will be most prominent when the Moon is high in the sky. Choose a dark site away from streetlights and give your eyes 10–15 minutes to adapt. Binoculars or a small telescope will reveal surface details that stand out against the dimmed glow. If clouds are forecast, have a backup plan to watch live streams from observatories. Finally, note that a lunar eclipse is safe to view directly—no eye protection is required.
