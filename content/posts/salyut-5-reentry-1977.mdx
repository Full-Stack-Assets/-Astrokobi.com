On August 8 1977, the Soviet Union’s Salyut 5 reconnaissance space station made its final descent, blazing through the atmosphere and disintegrating. Launched in June 1976 as part of the secret Almaz military program, the station had hosted three crews and returned valuable Earth‑observation film. By mid‑1977 its propellant reserves were exhausted, leaving it unable to maintain orbit. Soviet engineers therefore commanded a controlled deorbit, and the station burned up over the Pacific. The event marked the end of the Almaz‑derived Salyut series and closed a chapter of Cold‑War space espionage.

<Callout type="takeaway">Salyut 5’s uncontrolled re‑entry underscored the logistical limits of early military space stations.</Callout>

## What happened
Salyut 5 launched in June 1976 and was the third Almaz‑derived station dedicated to Earth reconnaissance. The first crew, Soyuz 21, arrived a month later but returned early because a noxious smell—likely a fuel leak—forced an abort. A second crew on Soyuz 23 failed to dock in October 1976 after a rendezvous system malfunction and returned to Earth, becoming the only Soyuz to splash down in water. The third and final crew, Soyuz 24, docked in February 1977, vented the station’s air to avoid contamination, and spent about 18 days aboard, after which a small re‑entry capsule carrying reconnaissance film separated and landed safely.

By mid‑1977 the station’s propellant for its main engines and attitude‑control system was critically low. Because Salyut 5 could not be refueled in orbit, Soviet controllers ordered a deorbit burn. The station’s orbit decayed, and it re‑entered the atmosphere, burning up over the Pacific Ocean. The event marked the final mission of the Almaz‑derived Salyut series.

## Why it matters
Salyut 5’s fate highlights the logistical constraints of early crewed military stations, where limited propellant and lack of on‑orbit refueling capped mission length. The program’s abrupt end forced Soviet planners to reconsider the sustainability of long‑duration orbital platforms, influencing the design of later stations like Mir, which incorporated more robust resupply capabilities. Moreover, the reconnaissance data gathered demonstrated the strategic value of crewed Earth‑observation, a concept that later shifted to unmanned satellites but laid groundwork for modern imaging constellations.

## How to think about it
When assessing historic space stations, weigh three factors: (1) logistical support (propellant, consumables, resupply), (2) mission objectives (scientific, military, commercial), and (3) geopolitical context that drives design priorities. Applying this framework helps explain why Salyut 5 succeeded in its short term but could not be sustained, and why later stations shifted toward modularity and regular resupply.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume Salyut 5’s deorbit was a routine end‑of‑life maneuver; it was forced by an unavoidable propellant shortage.</Callout>
