The Cottonwood Fire, one of Utah's largest and most destructive wildfires of the year, recently ravaged over 150 square miles of terrain, including parts of a ski resort. Fueled by a dry winter and warm summer, this blaze underscores the escalating wildfire risk across the western U.S. The event highlights the critical role of advanced satellite monitoring systems, like NASA's Fire Events Data Suite (FEDS), in tracking such rapidly evolving disasters and informing emergency responses.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Cottonwood Fire exemplifies the increasing threat of wildfires in the western U.S., where satellite technology is becoming indispensable for rapid detection and real-time tracking.</Callout>

## What happened
The Cottonwood Fire, as of early July 2026, had consumed approximately 150 square miles (390 square kilometers) in Utah, making it one of the largest fires in the state that year. The inferno swept through diverse vegetation, including ponderosa pine, oak, sagebrush, and grasses, leaving behind extensive blackened areas visible in satellite imagery from Landsat 9. Beyond natural landscapes, the fire caused significant structural damage, destroying up to 150 structures, including over 100 condos and 30 cabins at Eagle Point Ski Resort, which also reported damage to four of its five chairlifts.

The rapid progression of the fire was closely monitored by NASA's Fire Events Data Suite (FEDS), which utilizes VIIRS sensor data from satellites like Suomi NPP and NOAA-20/21. This system tracked the fire's surge, noting a tripling in size over just 12 hours on June 23 and another rapid expansion on June 26. FEDS provides consistent, easily accessible data, often detecting fire starts earlier than other sources and tracking blazes for their full duration, proving a valuable tool for firefighters and emergency management officials. Despite the widespread destruction, some areas, such as the Tushar Campground, survived due to prior forest treatments like brush clearing and branch trimming.

## Why it matters
This event is a stark reminder of the heightened wildfire risk facing the Great Basin and Rocky Mountain regions, driven by factors like below-average snowpack and unusually warm, dry conditions. The extensive damage to both natural ecosystems and human infrastructure, particularly the destruction of residential and resort properties, illustrates the profound economic and social impact these fires inflict. It underscores the urgent need for proactive forest management strategies, like those credited with saving the Tushar Campground, to build resilience against future blazes.

Furthermore, the Cottonwood Fire demonstrates the growing reliance on sophisticated satellite technology for disaster response. Tools like FEDS offer critical real-time intelligence, enabling firefighters to better understand fire behavior, anticipate spread, and allocate resources more effectively. As climate patterns continue to shift, leading to more frequent and intense fire seasons, the integration of such advanced monitoring systems becomes increasingly vital for protecting communities and natural resources across vulnerable regions.
