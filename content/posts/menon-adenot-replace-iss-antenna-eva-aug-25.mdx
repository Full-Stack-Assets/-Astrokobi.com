At 8:35 a.m. EDT on August 25, NASA astronaut Anil Menon and ESA astronaut Sophie Adenot began a 6.5‑hour spacewalk to replace a failed space‑to‑ground antenna on the International Space Station. The antenna, a critical relay for high‑speed communications with Mission Control, had been removed during a previous EVA on Aug 18 but a replacement was not installed. Today’s extravehicular activity, the 283rd EVA in ISS history, aims to restore that link and may also include a get‑ahead task to swap a retroreflector on the Harmony module. Successful completion will keep the station’s data flow robust as crews continue rotating through the orbiting laboratory. Watching the live feed gives space enthusiasts a front‑row seat to a routine yet vital maintenance operation.

<Callout type="takeaway">The EVA restores a vital communications link, keeping the ISS fully connected to Mission Control.</Callout>

## What happened
Menon, crewmember 1, and Adenot, crewmember 2, exited the Quest airlock at 8:35 a.m. EDT and headed to the station’s forward port where the faulty antenna resides. Using the Canadarm2 as a work platform, they detached the malfunctioning unit and secured the new antenna in its place. The operation proceeded according to the timeline outlined by NASA, and the crew reported nominal status throughout the primary task.

Because the EVA was scheduled after the earlier attempt on Aug 18, the astronauts had pre‑staged the replacement hardware and rehearsed the procedure during training. If time permitted, they also planned to replace a retroreflector on the Harmony module, a component that improves navigation data for visiting spacecraft. The spacewalk is slated to conclude around 3:05 p.m. EDT, marking a total duration of roughly 6.5 hours.

## Why it matters
A functional space‑to‑ground antenna is essential for transmitting high‑bandwidth science data, video, and crew health information back to Earth. Without it, the ISS would rely on slower, lower‑capacity communication pathways, potentially delaying critical mission updates. Restoring the link also supports upcoming experiments and ensures that future visiting vehicles can receive accurate navigation cues, reinforcing the station’s role as a hub for international research.
