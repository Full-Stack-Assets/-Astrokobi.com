The future of Boeing’s CST‑100 Starliner remains in limbo as NASA evaluates when to fly the cargo‑only Starliner‑1 mission. At a July 30 briefing, NASA’s Commercial Low‑Earth Orbit program deputy manager said progress on propulsion upgrades is “good,” but no launch window has been set. The mission, originally slated for early 2026, is now targeted for the fourth quarter of this year at the earliest. With the International Space Station’s retirement planned for 2030, each remaining Starliner flight is critical to meeting contract obligations. Stakeholders are watching closely to see if the spacecraft can secure a slot on the busy ISS manifest.

<Callout type="takeaway">Starliner‑1 will not fly until NASA and Boeing secure a launch slot and confirm the vehicle’s upgrades.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA’s Bill Spetch told reporters that the agency is working “very closely” with Boeing to address propulsion system issues identified after the crewed test flight two years ago, describing progress as “good.” Boeing’s president Kelly Ortberg echoed this sentiment on a July 28 earnings call, noting that the main hurdle now is aligning launch schedules for both crewed and uncrewed Starliner flights.

Boeing’s SEC filing indicates that Starliner‑1, a cargo‑only test flight, is planned for no earlier than the fourth quarter of 2026. The mission was absent from the ISS manifest presented at the briefing, which listed upcoming SpaceX crew and cargo flights and a Northrop Grumman Cygnus launch, suggesting Starliner‑1 still lacks an assigned launch slot.

A successful Starliner‑1 by year‑end would pave the way for the spacecraft’s first long‑duration crew rotation in late 2027, a crucial step before the ISS retirement deadline.

## Why it matters
The timing of Starliner‑1 directly impacts Boeing’s ability to fulfill the remaining two contracted missions before the ISS is decommissioned. Delays compress an already tight schedule, increase the risk of contract penalties, and reduce redundancy in U.S. low‑Earth‑orbit transport options. Conversely, a timely flight would validate recent propulsion and software upgrades, restoring confidence in the commercial crew program and preserving a domestic alternative to SpaceX’s Dragon.
