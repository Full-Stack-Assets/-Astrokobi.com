On August 6, solar observers confirmed that a series of coronal mass ejections (CMEs) launched from the Sun on August 3‑4 are on a trajectory toward Earth. The first impact is expected on August 7, with subsequent arrivals through August 9. These eruptions are classified as weak and faint, but their combined effect with a fast solar‑wind stream from a coronal hole could elevate geomagnetic activity to the G1 (minor) level. Even modest storms can brighten auroras at mid‑latitudes and affect satellite operations, making the upcoming interval a key watch period for both scientists and hobbyists.

<Callout type="takeaway">A chain of weak CMEs will brush Earth from August 7‑9, potentially sparking minor geomagnetic storms.</Callout>

## What happened
The Sun emitted several faint CMEs beginning on August 3, followed by additional eruptions on August 4 and August 5. Over the past day the Sun produced only B‑class flares, five in total, with the strongest being a B9.2 flare from active region AR4498 at 15:30 UTC on August 5. Four numbered active regions (AR4498‑AR4503) are visible, all in beta configuration and showing signs of decay.

Solar wind measurements indicate moderate to low speeds, and no significant space‑weather events were reported aside from the CME stream. Model visualizations show multiple clouds of solar plasma converging on Earth, suggesting a series of glancing blows that could persist until August 9.

## Why it matters
A G1 geomagnetic storm can produce visible auroras as far south as the northern United States and central Europe, offering a spectacular sky‑watching opportunity. At the same time, even minor storms increase the risk of satellite surface charging, can cause brief HF radio and GPS disruptions, and raise atmospheric drag on low‑Earth‑orbit spacecraft, affecting orbital predictions.

## How to think about it
Monitor official space‑weather alerts from NOAA and the Space Weather Prediction Center throughout the August 7‑9 window. If you operate sensitive satellite equipment, consider placing it in safe mode during peak geomagnetic activity. For sky‑watchers, plan to be in a dark location with clear horizons to maximize aurora viewing chances.

<Callout type="warning">Never look at the Sun with the naked eye or ordinary sunglasses; always use certified solar filters.</Callout>
