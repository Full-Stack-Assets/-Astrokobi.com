The Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program is poised for a busy finish to 2026, with NASA signaling three additional task orders before the year closes. Executives from Firefly Aerospace and Intuitive Machines told investors that two of those orders will fund new lander missions, while the third will fund an "orbiter imaging service" to succeed the aging Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO). The prospect of a next‑generation orbiter matters because LRO, launched in 2009, has been the workhorse for high‑resolution lunar imaging and is only slated to operate through 2028. Replacing it now would keep a continuous stream of data for science and future crewed missions. Commercial partners are already positioning themselves to deliver the successor, underscoring the growing role of private industry in sustained lunar exploration.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA plans to award three new CLPS task orders this year, including a long‑overdue orbiter to replace LRO.</Callout>

## What happened
In earnings calls on Aug. 12 and Aug. 14, Firefly Aerospace’s CEO Jason Kim and Intuitive Machines’ CEO Steve Altemus each confirmed that NASA expects to issue three more CLPS task orders by the end of 2026. Two of those orders will fund lander missions, continuing the trend that landers have comprised the bulk of CLPS work since the program’s inception in 2019. The third order is described as an "orbiter imaging service," intended to map the lunar surface in place of the LRO.

The LRO, launched in 2009 as part of the Vision for Space Exploration, still provides high‑resolution camera data and a suite of scientific instruments. Although it remains functional, the spacecraft is aging, and a 2022 Lunar Exploration Analysis Group report called for a next‑generation, long‑lived orbiter with modern instruments. NASA has not publicly detailed how it will develop that successor, nor has it released a source‑selection statement for the upcoming task orders.

## Why it matters
A new orbiter would preserve the continuity of lunar cartography, which is essential for both scientific research and the navigation of future lander and crewed missions. Commercial delivery of the orbiter could accelerate development timelines and reduce costs compared with a traditional NASA‑led build. Moreover, the data stream from a modern orbiter would likely include updated spectral, topographic, and radiation measurements, enriching the dataset that planners rely on for site selection and hazard assessment.

## How to think about it
For researchers, the best approach is to align upcoming proposals with the capabilities a next‑generation orbiter is likely to offer—high‑resolution imaging, multispectral analysis, and radiation monitoring. Policy makers should monitor the CLPS award timeline and consider how the orbiter’s data products can be integrated into Artemis landing site selection workflows. Industry participants can use the pending task orders as a signal to refine their lunar payload designs and to engage early with NASA’s procurement offices.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the orbiter will launch before the end of 2028; NASA has not released an official schedule, and contract negotiations may shift timelines.</Callout>
