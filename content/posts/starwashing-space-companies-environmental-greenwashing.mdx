Space’s rapid commercial growth is accompanied by a new branding trend that cloaks environmental harm in lofty language. Researchers have labeled this practice “starwashing,” a spin on greenwashing that frames space ventures as benefits to humanity while downplaying Earth and orbital damage. The term emerged alongside a projected $1.8 trillion space economy by 2035, highlighting the scale of the narrative. Companies such as Blue Origin publicly pledge to “restore and sustain Earth” even as their launch activities generate emissions and debris. Understanding starwashing is crucial for anyone who cares about both space exploration and planetary health.

<Callout type="takeaway">Starwashing lets space firms appear eco‑friendly while masking real environmental costs.</Callout>

## What happened
Starwashing describes a marketing playbook where space companies present their activities as inherently beneficial to humanity and the planet, while omitting or minimizing the associated environmental impacts. The concept was introduced by a social‑work researcher who observed that the language of cosmic wonder is being leveraged to distract from the carbon footprint of launches, the production of rocket components, and the creation of orbital debris.

The practice is already evident in corporate messaging. Blue Origin, for example, includes “restore and sustain Earth” in its mission statement even as its rockets emit greenhouse gases and contribute to the growing problem of space junk. The broader industry is projected to reach a $1.8 trillion valuation by 2035, amplifying the reach of these sustainability narratives.

## Why it matters
When environmental claims go unchecked, policy makers and the public may underestimate the true cost of expanding the space economy. Carbon emissions from launches add to climate change, while debris threatens satellite operations and future scientific missions. Moreover, the veneer of sustainability can stall stricter regulations, allowing harmful practices to continue unchecked.

## How to think about it
Treat every sustainability claim as a hypothesis, not a fact. Look for third‑party lifecycle assessments, disclosed emission data, and concrete mitigation plans. Support companies that publish transparent metrics and invest in reusable launch systems or debris‑removal technologies. Advocate for policy that requires independent verification of environmental statements before marketing them as green.

<Callout type="warning">Not every space‑related sustainability claim is false; evaluate the evidence before dismissing all industry efforts.</Callout>
