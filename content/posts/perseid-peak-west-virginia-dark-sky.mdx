The Perseid meteor shower, a reliable summer sky show, reaches its peak on the nights of August 12 and 13 this year. While many eastern cities wrestle with bright streetlights, West Virginia’s rugged mountains and protected public lands keep the night sky remarkably dark. That combination makes the Mountain State one of the best places in the eastern United States to watch dozens of meteors streak across the sky. For campers, hikers, and night‑sky photographers, the timing also lines up with a popular late‑summer travel window.

<Callout type="takeaway">West Virginia offers some of the East’s darkest skies for a prime Perseid viewing experience.</Callout>

## What happened
The Perseids are caused by Earth passing through debris left by Comet Swift‑Tuttle, producing bright, fast meteors that often leave glowing trails. This year the shower builds throughout early August and peaks on the nights of Aug. 12‑13, when observers under dark conditions can expect dozens of meteors per hour, weather permitting.

West Virginia stands out because its interior remains largely free of the sprawling light domes that blanket much of the East Coast. National forests, state parks, and wildlife areas cover millions of acres, and high‑elevation sites such as Spruce Knob (4,863 ft) sit far from urban centers, creating some of the darkest natural nightscapes east of the Mississippi.

## Why it matters
The convergence of a premier meteor shower with one of the nation’s darkest eastern skies boosts tourism for rural communities and highlights the value of preserving dark‑sky areas. Astronomers and photographers gain a rare opportunity to capture high‑quality meteor imagery without traveling to the West Coast. Moreover, the event raises public awareness of light‑pollution impacts and can inspire local conservation efforts.

## How to think about it
Pick a site with the highest elevation and the least nearby development—Spruce Knob, the Monongahela National Forest, or the New River Gorge are top choices. Check the weather forecast the day before and aim for the hours after local midnight when the radiant is highest. Arrive early to set up a comfortable viewing spot, use a red‑light flashlight to preserve night vision, and bring warm layers even in summer. If you plan to photograph, a wide‑field lens on a DSLR with a high ISO will capture the most meteors.

<Callout type="warning">Do not drive on unpaved mountain roads after dark without proper lighting and a vehicle suited for rugged terrain.</Callout>
