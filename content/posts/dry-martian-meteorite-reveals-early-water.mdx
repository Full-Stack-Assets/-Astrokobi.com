Scientists have long inferred that early Mars hosted liquid water, but direct samples from its crust have been elusive. A recent study examined the Martian crust meteorite Northwest Africa 7034—nicknamed “Black Beauty”—using X‑ray and neutron tomography. The analysis uncovered hydrated minerals embedded within the rock, providing the first tangible evidence of a substantial water reservoir in the ancient Martian crust. This discovery reshapes our view of the Red Planet’s early habitability and informs upcoming sample‑return missions.

<Callout type="takeaway">NWA 7034 contains hydrated minerals, confirming that early Mars had a crustal water reservoir.</Callout>

## What happened
Northwest Africa 7034 (NWA 7034), recovered on Earth and dated to about 4.4–4.5 billion years old, is the only known meteorite that originated from the Martian crust. Its provenance was established through isotopic signatures that match Martian atmospheric gases and surface rocks.

Researchers applied high‑resolution X‑ray tomography to map the mineral density and neutron tomography to detect hydrogen‑rich regions. The combined imaging revealed microscopic hydrated minerals and larger macroscopic hydrogen deposits that had been misidentified as voids in earlier X‑ray scans. These findings demonstrate that the rock retained water‑bearing phases since its ejection from Mars.

## Why it matters
The presence of hydrated minerals in a crustal sample directly ties early Martian water to the planet’s interior, suggesting that water was not confined to surface lakes or rivers but also percolated into the crust. This has implications for the planet’s thermal evolution, potential habitability, and the selection of future landing sites that aim to retrieve pristine ancient samples.

## How to think about it
When evaluating Mars’s early water budget, treat NWA 7034 as a proof‑of‑concept that crustal rocks can retain hydrous minerals over billions of years. Combine this insight with orbital spectroscopy and rover analyses to build a layered picture of water distribution. For educators, use the meteorite as a tangible example of how planetary scientists extract climate clues from extraterrestrial rocks.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the hydrated minerals represent the global water inventory of early Mars; they reflect conditions at a single location and may have been modified after landing on Earth.</Callout>
