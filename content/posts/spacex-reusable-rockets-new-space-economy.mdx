The launch landscape is shifting dramatically. Since SpaceX first landed a Falcon 9 booster upright in 2015, the company has turned rockets into reusable assets that can be reflown within weeks. This reuse has driven launch costs to roughly $1,500 per kilogram to low‑Earth orbit, a fraction of the historic “weight‑in‑gold” price tag. Today more than 5,000 objects are placed in orbit each year, and that number is on track to double annually, with SpaceX supplying the bulk of that payload mass. The ripple effect is a nascent commercial space economy that no longer depends on government‑only funding.

<Callout type="takeaway">Reusable rockets have turned space access from a rare, expensive privilege into a rapidly expanding commercial market.</Callout>

## What happened
In 2015 a Falcon 9 first‑stage booster performed a controlled landing after delivering its payload, proving that a launch vehicle could survive the trip and be recovered. Over the following years SpaceX refined the process, and today boosters are routinely reflown after brief inspections and refueling, dramatically shortening turnaround times.

The economic impact is evident: the cost to lift a kilogram to low‑Earth orbit has fallen to roughly $1,500, and annual launches now place over 5,000 objects into orbit, a figure that is expected to double each year. SpaceX accounts for the majority of that mass, and its Starlink broadband constellation generates about 70 percent of the company’s revenue, underscoring how commercial services are now funding further launch activity.

## Why it matters
Lower launch costs open the door for a wide range of actors—start‑ups, universities, and emerging nations—to access space without relying on costly government programs. This democratization accelerates satellite constellations, scientific missions, and in‑space manufacturing, reshaping the global economy and geopolitical balance.

At the same time, the rapid growth in launch frequency raises concerns about orbital congestion and debris, demanding new policies and tracking technologies. The concentration of launch capability in a single commercial entity also creates market risks if that provider faces technical or financial setbacks.

## How to think about it
When evaluating a space project, start by comparing the cost per kilogram of launch options; reusable vehicles now offer the most competitive rates. Next, assess the provider’s launch cadence and reliability record—frequent, successful flights reduce schedule risk. Finally, factor in the broader ecosystem: consider debris mitigation plans, regulatory compliance, and the diversification of launch partners to avoid over‑reliance on a single supplier.

<Callout type="warning">Do not ignore the growing risk of space debris; without active mitigation, increased traffic could jeopardize future missions.</Callout>
