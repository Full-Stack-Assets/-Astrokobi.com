The Milky Way’s early history just got a major update. NASA’s Hubble Space Telescope has captured definitive evidence that a dwarf galaxy, designated LKH, slammed into our young galaxy about 12 billion years ago. The finding appears in Nature Astronomy and pushes the earliest confirmed merger back by roughly 1.8 billion years. Understanding this collision helps astronomers map how the Milky Way grew from a fledgling disk into the massive spiral we see today. It also offers a rare glimpse of galaxy assembly in the universe’s first few billion years.

<Callout type="takeaway">A dwarf galaxy merger 12 billion years ago reshapes our timeline of the Milky Way’s formation.</Callout>

## What happened

Analysis of deep‑field Hubble imaging and spectroscopy identified a distinct stellar stream and chemical fingerprint that match the predicted remnants of the dwarf galaxy LKH. The data show a coherent motion and age distribution consistent with a collision occurring when the Milky Way was roughly 2 billion years old. Researchers used the stellar ages and metallicities to date the event to about 12 billion years ago, establishing it as the earliest confirmed merger in our galaxy’s record.

## Why it matters

Pinpointing such an early merger forces a revision of models that previously placed the Milky Way’s first major accretion later in cosmic time. It suggests that the young galaxy grew rapidly by assimilating smaller companions, influencing the formation of its disk, halo, and globular clusters. The result also provides a benchmark for simulations of galaxy formation, helping to align theoretical predictions with observable evidence.

## How to think about it

When evaluating the Milky Way’s past, treat this merger as a key data point rather than the sole driver of early evolution. Compare the LKH event with other known accretion signatures, such as the Gaia‑Sausage and the Sagittarius dwarf, to build a layered picture of growth. For hobbyist observers, consider how such ancient collisions manifest today as stellar streams and halo substructures that can be explored with publicly available sky surveys.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume this single merger defines the entire early history of the Milky Way; further observations are needed to confirm its broader impact.</Callout>
