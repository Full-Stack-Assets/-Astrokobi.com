Jupiter is the biggest planet in our Solar System, and its swirling atmosphere hosts the most iconic feature known to astronomers: the Great Red Spot. This anticyclonic storm has been raging for at least three centuries, with wind speeds that can reach 496 km/h. Its sheer scale—large enough to engulf two or three Earths—makes it a natural laboratory for studying planetary weather. Understanding the Spot helps scientists compare giant‑planet dynamics across the Solar System. The storm’s longevity also raises questions about how such features evolve over time.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Great Red Spot is a centuries‑old, planet‑wide storm that dominates Jupiter’s atmosphere and offers a unique window into giant‑planet weather.</Callout>

## What happened
The Great Red Spot is a persistent high‑pressure region located about 22° south of Jupiter’s equator. It is the largest anticyclonic vortex in the Solar System, producing wind speeds up to 496 km/h (308 mph) and appearing as a deep red‑orange oval that can engulf two or three Earths.

First recorded observations date to September 1831, and continuous monitoring began in 1879. Earlier mentions by Robert Hooke in 1664 and Giovanni Cassini between 1665 and 1713 may refer to a different feature, and a 2024 study suggests the modern Spot is not the same as the 17th‑century observations. Nonetheless, the storm has persisted for at least three hundred years, making it a remarkable example of long‑lived planetary weather.

## Why it matters
The Spot’s size and stability provide a rare chance to study atmospheric dynamics on a scale impossible on Earth. Its extreme winds and deep anticyclonic flow inform models of jet streams, heat transport, and cloud chemistry in hydrogen‑helium dominated atmospheres. By comparing Jupiter’s storm to transient features on Neptune, scientists can explore why some vortices endure for centuries while others fade within years, shedding light on the underlying physics of giant‑planet atmospheres.

## How to think about it
Treat the Great Red Spot as a benchmark case when evaluating any new Jovian or exoplanetary atmospheric data. First, assess the storm’s dimensions relative to planetary scale; then compare wind speeds to known jet streams. Next, consider chemical signatures—especially the unknown red hue—to gauge atmospheric composition. Finally, place the Spot within a timeline of observations to identify trends in size or intensity that may signal broader climatic shifts.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the Great Red Spot has existed continuously since the 17th‑century sightings; its precise lineage is still debated, and its color origin remains unresolved.</Callout>
