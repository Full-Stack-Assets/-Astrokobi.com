On Aug. 18, 2026, the Beijing‑based startup LandSpace successfully touched down the first stage of its Zhuque‑3 rocket on a landing pad in China. This marks the country's first land‑based recovery of an orbital‑class booster and only the second Chinese orbital rocket touchdown overall. The achievement follows a July 10 sea‑based landing of a Long March 10B and brings China closer to routine reusable launch operations. It demonstrates that private firms in China are now competing on the same reusability frontier as SpaceX.

<Callout type="takeaway">Zhuque‑3’s landing proves China can recover orbital boosters on land, a key step toward routine reusability.</Callout>

## What happened
LandSpace announced that the Zhuque‑3 first stage executed a controlled descent and came to rest upright on a purpose‑built landing pad, using four landing legs similar to those on SpaceX’s Falcon 9. The flight was the second Chinese orbital booster touchdown; the first, a Long March 10B, was captured by a net on a recovery ship on July 10.

The Zhuque‑3 is a 216‑foot (66 m) two‑stage vehicle capable of delivering about 18,300 kg to low‑Earth orbit, comparable in size to Falcon 9 but using a LOX‑methane (methalox) propellant mix rather than LOX‑kerosene. Its successful landing validates the reusable booster design and the engineering maturity of the system.

## Why it matters
A land‑based recovery eliminates the need for costly sea‑based net systems and opens the path to faster turnaround times, potentially lowering launch costs for Chinese customers. The milestone also signals that private Chinese launch providers are mastering technologies that have been dominated by a few Western companies, which could diversify the global launch market. Finally, the test moves Zhuque‑3 from pure demonstration toward an engineering‑application phase where regular re‑flights become feasible.

## How to think about it
For enthusiasts and analysts, treat this landing as a proof‑of‑concept rather than a sign of immediate operational reusability. Track LandSpace’s upcoming test flights to gauge how quickly they can certify a reused booster. Consider the broader market impact: if China can offer competitively priced, reusable launch services, it may shift payload allocation decisions worldwide.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that regular booster re‑flights are imminent; the technology is still in a verification phase and requires additional flight data.</Callout>
