Astronomers using JWST have uncovered a hidden population of faint, low‑mass stars in some of the universe’s earliest massive galaxies. By combining ultra‑deep JWST spectra with earlier Very Large Telescope data, the team measured the stellar makeup of nine mature galaxies that stopped forming stars billions of years ago. The analysis shows that these galaxies contain far more low‑mass stars than standard models assumed. Because low‑mass stars contribute disproportionately to a galaxy’s total mass, the galaxies are likely far heavier than earlier estimates indicated. This finding reshapes our view of how the first massive galaxies assembled in the early cosmos.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hidden low‑mass stars make early massive galaxies considerably heavier than previously measured.</Callout>

## What happened
The international team, led by researchers at Leiden University, targeted nine massive, quiescent galaxies at redshifts corresponding to when the universe was only a few billion years old. They obtained extremely deep near‑infrared spectra with JWST’s NIRSpec instrument and combined these data with existing ground‑based observations from the Very Large Telescope to achieve unprecedented signal‑to‑noise ratios.

By dissecting each galaxy’s light into a spectrum, the astronomers examined subtle color variations that betray the presence of different stellar types. While bright, massive stars dominate the visible light, careful modeling of the faint spectral features revealed a substantial contribution from low‑mass, long‑lived stars that had previously been invisible.

These findings challenge the long‑standing assumption that the initial mass function (IMF) – the relative numbers of stars of different masses – is universal. Instead, the early massive galaxies appear to have an IMF skewed toward a higher fraction of low‑mass stars, implying a larger total stellar mass than standard IMF‑based estimates.

## Why it matters
If early massive galaxies are significantly heavier, their rapid assembly in the first few billion years becomes easier to reconcile with cosmological simulations. Heavier galaxies also affect estimates of dark‑matter content, star‑formation efficiency, and the timeline of chemical enrichment. Moreover, the result forces a re‑examination of the IMF’s universality, a cornerstone of many astrophysical calculations.

## How to think about it
When evaluating the mass of distant galaxies, consider that standard IMF assumptions may underestimate the contribution of low‑mass stars. Incorporate a range of possible IMF shapes into mass‑budget calculations, especially for quiescent, massive systems. Use JWST spectral data to look for subtle absorption features that signal faint stellar components, and treat mass estimates as probabilistic rather than fixed values.

<Callout type="warning">The study’s conclusions are based on a small, carefully selected sample and on sophisticated spectral modeling; broader surveys are needed before revising mass estimates for all early massive galaxies.</Callout>
