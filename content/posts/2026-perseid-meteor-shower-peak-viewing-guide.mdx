Tonight the 2026 Perseid meteor shower officially begins, and astronomers expect the peak to arrive during the night of Aug. 12‑13. During the peak, the American Meteor Society predicts as many as 100 meteors per hour streaking across the sky. That same night the Moon will be in total eclipse, plunging a swath of the United States into darkness and creating an unusually dark backdrop for the display. For stargazers, the combination of a new‑moon sky, a total solar eclipse, and a high‑rate Perseid outburst makes this one of the most favorable viewing windows of the decade.

## What happened
The Perseid stream, shed by comet Swift‑Tuttle, begins intersecting Earth’s orbit tonight and reaches its densest point on the night of Aug. 12‑13. According to the American Meteor Society, observers under clear, dark skies can expect up to 100 meteors per hour, with many bright fireballs that can outshine Venus.

The radiant lies near the red supergiant Eta Persei in the constellation Perseus. After the evening twilight fades, look toward the north‑western horizon and then tilt your view about 40° above the radiant; this is where the longest trails appear. Perseids are known for vivid green fireballs that travel at roughly 37 miles per second (59 km/s), and the rate climbs in the pre‑dawn hours as the radiant climbs higher.

Because the peak coincides with a total solar eclipse on Aug. 12, observers located in the path of totality will experience an extra few minutes of near‑total darkness, potentially enhancing fireball visibility. However, the odds of being directly under the eclipse’s path while a large meteoroid enters the atmosphere are low.

## Why it matters
A meteor shower of this intensity under eclipse‑darkened skies provides a unique natural laboratory for both casual observers and professional researchers studying meteoroid fragmentation and atmospheric entry dynamics. The high fireball rate also offers a rare opportunity to capture bright, fast‑moving events on video, contributing valuable data to the International Meteor Organization.

For the public, the event blends two spectacular phenomena—meteor showers and eclipses—into a single night, boosting interest in night‑sky observation and encouraging outreach programs that promote dark‑sky preservation.

## How to think about it
Find a dark site at least 30 minutes away from city lights and allow your eyes to adapt for 20 minutes before looking up. Locate Perseus low on the north‑western horizon, then point your view roughly 40° above that point to catch the longest streaks. The best window is from midnight until just before dawn, when the radiant is highest. Bring a reclining chair, a warm blanket, and a red‑light flashlight to preserve night vision. No telescopes are needed—just a wide‑field view; a smartphone camera on a tripod works well for recording fireballs.

<Callout type="warning">Never look directly at the Sun during the eclipse without proper eye protection.</Callout>
