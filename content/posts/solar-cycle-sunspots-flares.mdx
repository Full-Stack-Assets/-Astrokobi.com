Every 11 years the Sun goes through a magnetic heartbeat known as the solar cycle, a rhythm that governs the rise and fall of sunspots and the frequency of powerful solar flares. During the peak of a cycle, the Sun’s magnetic field becomes tangled, spawning bright sunspots that can unleash flares and coronal mass ejections (CMEs) into space. The most dramatic reminder of this process arrived on 1–2 September 1859, when a flare and CME produced the Carrington Event, the strongest geomagnetic storm on record. Modern satellites such as the Solar Maximum Mission have watched these eruptions up close, revealing how the hot corona and its magnetic structures drive space weather. Understanding this chain of events is crucial because a similar storm today could cripple power grids, communications, and navigation systems.

<Callout type="takeaway">The solar cycle’s magnetic turbulence links sunspots, flares, and Earth‑impacting storms.</Callout>

## What happened
The Sun’s 11‑year magnetic cycle flips the polarity of its global field, causing magnetic flux tubes to emerge as dark sunspots on the photosphere. When these tubes become twisted, they release energy as solar flares and launch CMEs that race outward through the corona.

On 1 September 1859 British astronomers recorded a bright white‑light flare, and a CME followed, covering the 150×10⁶ km distance to Earth in just 17.6 hours. Ground magnetometers measured a storm‑time disturbance (Dst) between –0.80 and –1.75 μT, producing auroras visible at low latitudes and sparking fires in telegraph networks.

The solar corona, the Sun’s outer atmosphere, sits above the photosphere at temperatures exceeding 1 000 000 K, far hotter than the surface. Its structure—loops, prominences, and helmet streamers—is shaped by the magnetic field and extends to the Alfvén surface 10–20 solar radii (7–14 million km) where the solar wind breaks free. The Solar Maximum Mission, launched on 14 February 1980, carried a coronagraph and X‑ray instruments that captured dozens of flares, providing the first systematic view of how coronal dynamics feed CMEs.

## Why it matters
When a CME collides with Earth’s magnetosphere, it can induce powerful electric currents that overload power grids, damage satellites, and expose astronauts to heightened radiation. The Carrington Event shows that a storm of similar magnitude today would threaten modern infrastructure on a global scale. Continuous monitoring of sunspot numbers, flare classifications, and coronal imagery therefore underpins space‑weather forecasting, which helps utilities, airlines, and mission planners mitigate risk.

## How to think about it
Treat the solar cycle as a weather forecast: monitor the sunspot number, note the class of any flare (C, M, X), and watch coronagraph images for Earth‑directed CMEs. When a fast CME is observed, follow space‑weather alerts from agencies such as NOAA and ESA, and activate protective measures—grid operators can re‑configure transformers, satellite operators can place assets in safe mode, and mission planners can adjust extravehicular activities.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a low sunspot count guarantees safety; even during solar minimum, fast CMEs can occur and still cause significant disturbances.</Callout>
